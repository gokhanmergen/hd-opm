Phillips: 74 trials show no extra muscle gain above 1.6 g/kg/day protein with lifting

Mar 28, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 8 · 💬 12 ↩ 5 Protein

Louis shares Phillips's own summary: a pooled analysis of 74 RCTs (nearly 2,700 people) found that beyond about 1.6 g/kg/day with resistance training, more protein adds nothing. In a 40% calorie deficit, 2.4 g/kg helped a little with lean mass, but 1.2 g/kg still preserved muscle.

Stuart Phillips: the 1.6 g/kg protein ceiling holds for older adults; women may need less

Mar 29, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 11 · 💬 6 ↩ 5 Protein

Louis shares part 2 of Stuart Phillips's protein rebuttal. In older adults the meta-analysis found lean mass gains at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day and no trials above 1.6 g/kg; in older men, raising protein from 0.8 to about 1.3 g/kg raised muscle protein synthesis about 9%, while collagen did nothing.

Protein above 1.6 g/kg adds no benefit even in older adults who lift, says Stuart Phillips

Review of 33 systematic studies finds little evidence omega-3 supplements build muscle

Mar 14, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 11 · 💬 2 Supplements & vitamins

Louis shares Stuart Phillips' summary of a 2025 Clinical Nutrition ESPEN review of 33 systematic studies, which found little evidence that omega-3 supplements improve lean mass, muscle strength or physical function in healthy adults or clinical groups. Some small benefits in older adults were suggested, but exercise and protein remain the best strategies.

Pre- and postmenopausal women gain strength equally from resistance training

Apr 16, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 7 · 💬 2 Exercise & fitness

A 2026 JSAMS meta-analysis of randomized trials found women gain strength at similar rates across the lifespan, suggesting estrogen loss at menopause does not blunt response to resistance training. Louis adds that HRT alone does not add muscle.

Stuart Phillips: 1.6 g/kg/day protein remains sufficient for people who resistance train

Nov 06, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 5 Protein

Louis shares a response from protein researcher Stuart Phillips defending the 1.6 g/kg/day recommendation after a critique. He argues it is a sufficiency with a margin, not a cliff, and that head-to-head trials of 1.6 versus up to 3.2 g/kg/day found no significant lean-mass advantage, while three studies cited for more benefit used weak muscle measures.

Centenarians' higher LDL reflects survivorship bias, not a benefit of high LDL

Oct 15, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 8 · 💬 36 LDL, ApoB & lipid panel

Louis explains that centenarians tend to have higher LDL simply because higher LDL is common in the general population; they reach 100 because of protective genes that let them survive despite high LDL. Low LDL is uncommon in Western countries, genetics is the main determinant of reaching 100, and low LDL may delay cardiovascular death but will not by itself get someone to 100.

Low LDL in cancer patients reflects reverse causality: tumours scavenge LDL

Mendelian randomization: lifelong lower LDL tracks linearly with lower heart disease risk

Oct 10, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 13 · 💬 8 LDL, ApoB & lipid panel

The post explains a chart from a European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement showing that genetic variants affecting LDL through different pathways (LDLR, PCSK9, APOB, HMGCR, NPC1L1, SORT1, ABCG5/8) change coronary risk in proportion to the LDL change. This matches the linear relationship seen in statin trials. The poster also shows how to convert relative risk reductions into lifetime absolute risk.

Louis quotes a Barbell Medicine explanation that low cholesterol in sick, older people reflects disease lowering cholesterol, not the reverse. Genetically low LDL lowers heart disease with normal cancer risk and lifespan, and lowering LDL on purpose cuts heart risk proportionally. The small diabetes risk is concentrated in people already near the metabolic edge.

U-shaped ApoB-mortality curve turns linear after adjusting for bilirubin and malnutrition

Jun 01, 2024 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 13 · 💬 5 ↩ 1 LDL, ApoB & lipid panel

Louis explains a 2022 Chinese cohort of about 36,500 people showing the commonly observed U-shaped relationship between ApoB and all-cause mortality disappears once comorbidities, bilirubin and malnutrition are corrected for. Low ApoB at older ages appears to be a consequence of illness and wasting rather than a cause of death, consistent with RCT and Mendelian randomization evidence that the low-end increase is not causal.

Louis explains why observational studies linking low LDL to cancer are not causal. Citing a Circulation review on reverse causality, he notes cholesterol declines before cancer diagnosis (likely from cancer-related inflammation), statin RCTs show no increase in cancer, and Mendelian randomization with an average of 52 years of lifetime exposure shows no higher cancer rates in people with genetically lower cholesterol.

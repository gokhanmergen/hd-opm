Questions newcomers ask

Tap a question for the short answer, then read the full research summary or the group's key post.

Getting started

Where do I start?

Plaque builds silently for decades as LDL and other ApoB particles get trapped in artery walls. In the REACT study, 57% of adults aged 18–70 already had some.

Read the answer →Key post
Which risk factors matter most?

Age is by far the biggest. Of the ones you can change, having none of the classic five (blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, diabetes, smoking) at 50 meant up to 13 more years free of heart disease.

Read the answer →Key post
What numbers should I aim for?

LDL goals scale with your risk: under 100 mg/dL at intermediate risk, under 70 at high risk and under 55 at very high risk. ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure and blood sugar complete the picture.

Read the answer →Key post
How do I estimate my own risk?

The AHA PREVENT calculator estimates 10- and 30-year risk for ages 30–79. It underestimates risk if you already have plaque or a high calcium score.

Read the answer →Key post
How do I find a good cardiologist?

Rule #1 at high risk: find a prevention-minded cardiologist and listen to them. Add lifestyle and supplements on top of treatment, not instead of it.

Read the answer →Key post

Cholesterol & LDL

How low should LDL go, and how early?

Lower and longer both count. The same LDL drop cuts heart-attack risk by about 20% in 5-year trials, but by over 50% when it lasts for decades.

Read the answer →Key post
Should I test ApoB instead of LDL?

ApoB counts every artery-clogging particle, while LDL-C only measures the cholesterol inside them. When the two disagree, risk follows ApoB.

Read the answer →▶ Watch the video
Isn't low cholesterol dangerous?

No. In observational data, low cholesterol tracks illness rather than causing harm. People born with low LDL have less heart disease and normal lifespans.

Read the answer →▶ Watch the video
What is Lp(a), and should I test it?

An inherited, LDL-like particle that is high in about 1 in 5 people. It is 80–90% genetic and stable for life, so testing once as an adult is enough.

Read the answer →Key post
Do triglycerides and HDL matter?

Mostly as markers of insulin resistance. Drugs that raised HDL failed in every large trial, and ApoB already counts triglyceride-rich particles.

Read the answer →Key post

Statins & other medications

Are statins worth it?

Across 25 trials, lowering LDL by about 75 mg/dL cut heart attacks and strokes by about 40%. Imaging trials show soft plaque shrinking too.

Read the answer →Key post
Are statin side effects real?

Real but uncommon. In blinded trials, muscle aches were almost as common on placebo (26.6%) as on a statin (27.1%).

Read the answer →▶ Watch the video
What if I can't tolerate a statin?

Try a lower dose or a different statin before quitting. Ezetimibe or bempedoic acid can make up the difference.

Read the answer →Key post
Should I take a daily aspirin?

With little or no plaque, the small benefit is offset by bleeding risk. It is clearly used after a heart attack or with significant plaque.

Read the answer →▶ Watch the video
What blood pressure should I aim for?

Ideally under 120 mmHg systolic. In SPRINT, intensive control was estimated to add about 2.9 years of life from age 50.

Read the answer →Key post

Plaque & calcium scores

I have a high calcium score. What now?

It marks high risk, not a death sentence. The plan centres on driving LDL very low: under 55 mg/dL for a score above 300.

Read the answer →Key post
Does a calcium score of zero mean I'm safe?

Low risk, not no risk. A zero score can miss soft, non-calcified plaque: in one Danish registry, about half of patients with blockages scored zero.

Read the answer →Key post
Can plaque actually shrink?

Yes, mostly the soft, lipid-rich part. Shrinkage generally starts once LDL is below roughly 90 mg/dL, with more the lower it goes.

Read the answer →Key post
Why did my calcium score rise on a statin?

Often a sign of stabilization. Statins turn soft plaque into dense, stable calcium, which raises the score even as risk falls.

Read the answer →Key post
Should I get a CT angiogram?

It shows the soft plaque a calcium scan misses. In SCOT-HEART it led to more treatment and fewer heart attacks over five years.

Read the answer →Key post

Diet & lifestyle

What should I eat?

A Mediterranean-style pattern: vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, olive oil and fish. Keep red meat rare and processed food to a minimum.

Are seed oils bad for me?

The evidence says no. Higher blood linoleic acid tracks lower heart deaths, and trials show eating more of it doesn't raise inflammation markers.

Is keto or carnivore OK for my heart?

Strict keto often raises LDL, sometimes dramatically. The KETO-CTA study that seemed reassuring had no control group and was later retracted.

How much exercise do I need?

Start with walking: about 7,000 steps a day captured most of the benefit in one meta-analysis. Aim for 150+ minutes of activity a week plus strength training about twice a week.

How much does losing weight help?

Often the biggest single lever. The group cites about 30 lb (15 kg) as enough, on average, to clear liver fat and reverse insulin resistance.

Supplements

Which supplements actually have evidence?

Only a few have trial support for heart outcomes: omega-3, CoQ10 in heart failure and folic acid for stroke. Vitamins C and D showed no effect.

Does nattokinase dissolve plaque?

One study saw carotid plaque improve at 10,800 FU a day, but no trial shows fewer heart attacks, and it adds bleeding risk alongside blood thinners.

Can red yeast rice or berberine replace a statin?

Both lower LDL somewhat, but product quality is unregulated and neither has trials showing fewer heart attacks or strokes.

Should I take vitamin D or K2?

Large vitamin D trials mostly missed heart outcomes. K2 slowed calcium-score rises but hasn't been shown to cut heart attacks.

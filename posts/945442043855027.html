Calcium from a large glass of milk may lower bowel cancer risk ~20%; CVD effect debated

Louis Nervegna · Jan 10, 2025 · ♥ 11 reactions · 26 comments · original Facebook post
CancerSupplements & vitamins
AI summary & key facts

Louis shares a Cancer Research UK-funded Oxford study finding that the calcium in a large glass of milk daily could lower bowel cancer risk by nearly 20%, from dairy or non-dairy sources. Louis adds that excess calcium may contribute to CVD, which is controversial in cardiology.

  • Calcium equal to a large glass of milk per day: nearly 20% lower bowel cancer risk
  • Similar effect from dairy and non-dairy calcium
  • Largest study on diet and bowel cancer to date

Calcium is a complicated subject.

This is one of multiple studies that has strongly hinted at a protective effect against colon cancer.

But excess calcium consumption may contribute to CVD. Whether or not this is actually true is a controversial subject in cardiology.

#calciumHD #coloncancerHD #dairyHD

Research cited

  • Oxford Population Health diet and bowel cancer study (Cancer Research UK) · cohort
Shared from Cancer Research UK · original post

A new study we funded at the University of Oxford shows that having the amount of calcium in a large glass of milk every day could lower bowel cancer risk by nearly 20%.

In the largest study ever conducted on the links between diet and bowel cancer, calcium from both dairy and non-dairy sources had a similar effect.

These results help to highlight the importance of a healthy balanced diet in reducing cancer risk and supporting long-term health.

Research has found that more than half of bowel cancer cases are preventable. New findings, like this study by Oxford Population Health, are an important step towards ensuring fewer people are impacted by cancer in the future.

Amazing work from the University of Oxford 💜

NEJM 2020 dairy review: a mixed bag, best for fermented forms, weak for fractures

Jan 23, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 6 · 💬 21 Meat, eggs & dairy

Louis highlights the widely cited 2020 NEJM review on dairy and disease risk. It concludes that high dairy intake is not supported for preventing fractures, is not clearly linked to weight, diabetes or CVD, and may raise prostate and endometrial cancer risk while lowering colorectal cancer risk. Effects depend on the comparison food: dairy fares better than processed red meat or sugary drinks but worse than nuts.

Louis shares a summary suggesting full-fat cheese affects LDL less than butter or cream despite similar saturated fat.

In the meantime, I drink a glass of red wine several days per week.

Why do I do this?

Because there is now good evidence from the PREDIMED randomized controlled trial that mild red wine consumption enhances the cardio-protective effects of a traditional Mediterranean diet. People in PREDIMED with blood levels of a marker of red wine consumption reflective of mild to moderate consumption had 50% less risk than people with near zero levels.

↪ PREDIMED follow-up: urinary tartaric acid links mild-moderate wine to HR 0.5 for CVD

In fact, almost all the high quality studies showing a protective effect of a traditional Mediterranean diet against CVD included mild consumption of red wine. The diet might not work as well without the wine.

↪ Four long-term RCTs show the Mediterranean diet cuts CVD death by 46% in aggregate

↪ Meta-analysis of 4 Mediterranean diet RCTs: MACE down 48%, CVD death down 46%

There are over 100 prospective studies that associate mild alcohol consumption with reduced risk of CVD. It is associated with a 25-40% risk reduction in aggregate from all these studies, which is very large. So while alcohol may have become controversial amongst influencers and clinicians, the best epidemiologists at the Harvard School of Public Health are still convinced that it likely reduces risk for CVD.

↪ Harvard: 100+ cohorts show 25-40% lower CVD risk with light-to-moderate alcohol

Red wine is probably the best choice.

↪ Harvard epidemiologists consider red wine the best choice if you drink moderately

One reason why is because red wine does not seem to raise cancer risk as much as other types of alcohol, especially when consumed mildly. In fact, in mild amounts, it may not raise cancer risk at all, and might even be protective against some types of cancer. This evidence is observational, so it must be taken with a grain of salt— but it is the best that we have so far specifically on red wine.

↪ Meta-analysis of 76 studies finds no link between wine drinking and cancer risk

↪ Meta-analysis of 4.3 million: moderate wine not linked to higher overall cancer risk

The reason that alcohol has become controversial with regards to CVD is because of a couple recent Mendellian randomization studies which claim to show no protective effect. However, there are major problems with using Mendellian randomization to study alcohol and CVD, as explained in the paper below. The authors are faculty at Harvard Medical School and the Harvard School of Public Health.

We will finally see a large long-running randomized controlled trial that directly tests alcohol (red wine) verses cardiovascular disease.

The study will run for 5 years, and also report on secondary endpoints of cancer and all-cause mortality.

They are testing alcohol in this trial as if it were a medication. One group will be randomized to consume a set dose of wine each day, and the other group will be randomized to consume an alcohol-free alternative. Everything else will be the same between the two groups. Wine is the only variable.

The trial will be run in the same style as the famous PREDIMED trial that tested a traditional Mediterranean diet vs CVD. In fact, it will be run by the same principle investigator as PREDIMED.

Given the people involved, this should be a very high quality trial.

It will finally give us the truth on mild-to-moderate alcohol consumption and health, whatever that may be.

Quoted:

“Randomized controlled trials are needed to ensure that potential benefits or harms from alcohol consumption are adequately assessed, Martínez-González said. He and his colleagues at the University of Navarra received funding for such a study earlier this year. The study is recruiting physicians ages 50 to 75, who will be randomized to moderate drinking or abstention groups. Both groups will receive free beverages—red wine or an alcohol-free alternative—during the five-year study.”

“So, should we remove wine from the Mediterranean diet?” he asked. “Yes, definitely for those under 35 years.” But for older adults, it’s more complicated. He and his colleagues, and other researchers, have found that the Mediterranean diet loses up to 23.5% of its protective effect if wine is removed.”

Note:

The PREDIMED trial previously showed that a traditional Mediterranean diet including red wine lowered the risk of cardiovascular disease by 30%, as compared to a AHA-style diet that did not include alcohol. This was in healthy people who had never had a heart attack before the start of the trial, i.e. it was a primary prevention trial.

The people who consumed the most red wine in this trial (while not exceeding moderate levels) had 50% less risk of having a heart attack or stroke than the people who consumed the least. The researchers tracked wine consumption with a urinary biomarker that is unique to wine (tartaric acid). This is the most accurate way of tracking alcohol consumption ever used in a long term study to date: ↪ PREDIMED follow-up: urinary tartaric acid links mild-moderate wine to HR 0.5 for CVD.

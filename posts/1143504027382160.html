Influencers citing only 1960s-70s studies or post-hoc RCT analyses are likely misleading

Louis Nervegna · Oct 23, 2025 · ♥ 12 reactions · 3 comments · original Facebook post
How to read researchMyths, misinformation & controversies
AI summary & key facts

Louis warns that influencers often cite decades-old studies, ignoring 50 years of newer research, or present observational post-hoc analyses of randomized trials as if they were RCTs, which cannot prove causality. He names Paul Saladino as a frequent user of both tricks and urges readers to look up the references.

  • Ad hoc observational analyses of an RCT cannot prove causality
  • Such analyses must be judged in context of all observational research
  • Most people do not check cited references

If an influencer can only cite studies from the 1960’s and 1970’s to support their position, they are almost certainly BS’ing you.

You can’t pretend that the last 50 years of scientific research just didn’t happen— and ignore the hundreds of newer studies that occurred during that time period. It’s 2025, not 1985.

Another common trick that they use is to claim to cite a randomized controlled trial… but really cite a lower-quality observational analysis of a randomized trial done ad hoc, after the original trial is over. These observational analyses can NOT prove causality, just like any other observational study. You have to consider them in the context of all the observational research as a whole on the subject, not in isolation by themselves.

This is why you have to actually look up the references these people cite. Half the time, you find out that they are misleading you.

Paul Saladino uses these two tricks repeatedly. It is his modus operandi. Lots of other influencers do it as well. They get away with it because they know that 99% of people are not going to actually look up the references and check them.

Hypothesis: disrupted iron handling after SARS-CoV-2 infection may trigger long COVID

Louis Nervegna · Mar 07, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 5 comments · original Facebook post

Shares a news summary proposing that infection makes the body sequester iron, which if prolonged could reduce iron available for red blood cells and contribute to persistent symptoms. Louis calls it one of the more interesting long-COVID hypotheses; it is not yet clinically proven.

  • Immune response locks away free iron during infection
  • Prolonged iron dysregulation proposed to contribute to long COVID

Research cited

  • Iron dysregulation after SARS-CoV-2 and long COVID (News-Medical report) · other · News-Medical · 2026
Shared from Patrik Andersson · original post

The article discusses COVID-19 and how the infection can disrupt the body’s handling of iron, which may contribute to persistent symptoms after the infection.

When the body fights COVID-19, the immune system tries to reduce the amount of free iron in the blood. This is a defense mechanism because many microorganisms need iron to grow. The body therefore “locks away” iron in different tissues so that viruses and bacteria cannot easily use it.

The problem arises if this condition becomes long-lasting. Then less iron is available for red blood cells, which means oxygen is transported less efficiently throughout the body. This can affect metabolism and energy production. White blood cells also require iron to function normally. A mechanism that initially protects the body can therefore, over time, create new problems.

The researchers suggest that this may help explain why fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance are common symptoms in long COVID and other post-viral conditions.

The study also points to possible ways of reducing the risk of long-term symptoms. One approach may be to dampen the strong inflammation early during the COVID-19 infection, before it has time to disrupt the body’s iron regulation. Another possible avenue is treatment related to iron. However, the issue is often not that the body has too little iron, but that the iron becomes trapped in the wrong tissues instead of circulating in the blood where it is needed.

The researchers therefore believe that future treatments may need to focus on restoring iron to the bloodstream, so that red blood cells and immune cells can use it again.

The results are also indirectly supported by other studies. In the IRONMAN study, which primarily investigated iron treatment in heart failure, preliminary signals suggested that participants receiving iron had a lower risk of severe COVID-19 complications. Similar observations have been made in people with the blood disorder beta-thalassemia, where iron levels in the blood are often high.

Overall, the research suggests that disrupted iron regulation during and after COVID-19 may contribute to persistent symptoms, and that treatments restoring the body’s normal iron distribution could become important in the future.

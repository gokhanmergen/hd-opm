Long-term melatonin use linked to higher heart failure risk in an observational analysis

Louis Nervegna · Nov 03, 2025 · ♥ 25 reactions · 29 comments · original Facebook post
Supplements & vitaminsHeart rhythm & heart failure
AI summary & key facts

Louis comments on an AHA-presented analysis of more than 130,000 adults with chronic insomnia, in which melatonin use over a year was associated with about 90% higher odds of heart failure. He warns it is retrospective, cannot prove causation, and that melatonin is a hormone whose long-term high-dose effects are not well studied.

  • 130,000+ adults with chronic insomnia
  • ~90% higher odds of heart failure, 3x more heart failure hospitalization, nearly double mortality
  • Natural nightly production is about 200 micrograms; many take 10 mg or more
  • Retrospective observational: confounding and reverse causality possible

Melatonin is a hormone, not a harmless vitamin.

We don’t properly understand the long-term effects of taking super-physiological doses for long periods of time. It has not been properly studied long-term in humans.

(We do know that it is safe in the shorter term.)

The typical physiological dose that your body produces naturally at night is around 200 micrograms.

(I see people online taking anywhere from 10mg up to entire gram! This is 50x to 5000x times more than your body produces naturally.)

Keep in mind that this is a retrospective observational study. The association could be due to reverse causality or confounding, or one of the many other issues common in retrospective studies. This study does not prove causality.

Long-Term Melatonin Use Linked to Higher Heart Failure Risk

A major new analysis challenges the assumption that melatonin is risk-free.

Researchers reviewing global health data found that adults using melatonin for more than a year had roughly 90% higher odds of heart failure and were over three times more likely to be hospitalized for it.

The supplement’s long-term use was also tied to nearly double the risk of death.

Experts caution that while this study shows association, not causation, it underscores the need for medical guidance and better regulation of over-the-counter melatonin products.

Very small and uncontrolled; larger placebo-controlled trials are needed.

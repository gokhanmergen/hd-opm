Ornish diet versus traditional Mediterranean diet: key differences

Louis Nervegna · Aug 10, 2025 · ♥ 7 reactions · 13 comments · original Facebook post
Mediterranean, DASH, MIND & plant-forward diets
AI summary & key facts

Louis quotes a Cleveland Clinic comparison. Both emphasise plants and whole grains, but the Mediterranean diet permits olive oil, nuts, avocado, poultry, limited red meat and egg yolks, which the largely vegan Ornish diet excludes.

  • Ornish diet is primarily vegan with very limited fat
  • Mediterranean allows extra virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado, skinless poultry

Ornish diet vs. traditional Mediterranean diet

What are the differences?

Quoted:

“When it comes to weight loss and overall health, the Mediterranean diet is one of the best, Zumpano shares. It’s similar to the Ornish diet in that both center on fruits, vegetables, legumes and whole grains. Neither emphasizes counting or restricting calories.

But there are some key differences. For example, the [traditional] Mediterranean diet allows you to eat some foods that aren’t included in the Ornish diet. That includes foods like:

  • Healthy fats, such as extra virgin olive oil, seeds, nuts and avocados.
  • Meat, mainly skinless poultry.
  • Limited amounts of [unprocessed] red meat.
  • Egg yolks.
  • Moderate-fat [fermented] dairy products such as part-skim cheese, cottage cheese and yogurt.
  • Seafood, including a recommended three servings of fish per week.

On the other hand, the Mediterranean diet doesn’t specifically include the lifestyle aspects of the Ornish program. Although exercise is encouraged and social support is foundational to Mediterranean cultures.”

Ornish trial: lifestyle program cut cardiac events, but plant-based diet was not a cure

Jun 24, 2024 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 11 · 💬 20 ↩ 13 Mediterranean, DASH, MIND & plant-forward diets

In Ornish's small 1998 randomized trial, patients on the diet and lifestyle program had significantly fewer cardiac events over 5 years than controls (25 events in 28 patients vs 45 in 20 controls). Even among 28 who adhered, there were still 2 heart attacks, 8 angioplasties, 2 bypasses, 23 hospitalizations and 2 deaths, so the diet was not a cure.

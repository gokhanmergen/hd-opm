CGMs overestimated glucose by about 16 mg/dL in healthy adults in a crossover trial

Louis Nervegna · Dec 02, 2025 · ♥ 3 reactions · 1 comments · original Facebook post
Risk assessment & lab testsInsulin resistance & diabetes
AI summary & key facts

Louis shares a post on an AJCN randomized crossover trial in 15 healthy adults where CGM readings ran about 16 mg/dL higher than capillary samples fasting and after meals, overstated the glycemic index of fruit smoothies and exaggerated time above threshold. He advises trusting lab values over a disagreeing CGM.

  • 15 healthy adults
  • CGM about 16 mg/dL higher fasting and postprandial
  • Time above threshold overestimated about 4-fold unadjusted, 2-fold adjusted
  • Large person-to-person variability in bias

CGM’s have major accuracy problems in non-diabetics. They overestimate blood sugar values. Multiple studies have shown this now. If your lab measured values are all good, but your CGM does not agree— then don’t trust the CGM.

#cgmsHD #glucoseHD

Research cited

  • Continuous glucose monitor overestimates glycemia: a randomized crossover trial · RCT · Am J Clin Nutr · 2025
Shared from Daniel Chong · original post

One thing that has always made me uncomfortable is people without type one diabetes, using continuous glucose monitors as a way to monitor blood sugar responses to diet and adjust things based on the readings. This brand new study helps me feel like I wasn’t just being overly cautious.

What they found:

  • In 15 healthy adults, CGM readings averaged ~16mg/dL higher in both fasting and post-meal states compared with capillary vessel sampling. ￼
  • CGM also overestimated a glycaemic index of the fruit smoothies.
  • CGM also over-estimated the time spent above a glucose threshold ~4-fold unadjusted, and ~2-fold after adjustment for baseline differences! 😱
  • There was noticeable variability from person to person in how much the CGM over-estimated fasting glucose levels. ￼

What it means:

  • CGMs can be helpful tools for people with diabetes who need continual monitoring.
  • But for healthy people using CGMs to evaluate food responses or “optimize” glycaemia, these results suggest caution because the device may mis-characterize the magnitude of blood-glucose changes, and even the ranking (e.g., GI of foods) can be affected.

If you’re using a CGM to see how you react to certain foods, be aware that the device might show a bigger bump or longer “time above range” than is actually happening in your bloodstream. Don’t jump to conclusions like “this fruit smoothie triggered a huge spike” when in fact the data might be an over-estimate.

It’s also very important to mention that, at least in my experience, use of these devices in non-diabetes situations can lead to some people becoming overly cautious and concerned about what amount to totally normal rises in blood sugar that occur with eating carbohydrates.

If you are interested in monitoring your blood sugar and insulin sensitivity there are more accurate ways to do it.

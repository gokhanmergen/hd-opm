Esselstyn's diet evidence is case series, not RCTs; Mediterranean diet trials match it

Louis Nervegna · Mar 07, 2025 · ♥ 22 reactions · 48 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis argues that Esselstyn's published results are case-based observational studies at the bottom of the evidence hierarchy, in patients who were heavily medicated and mostly on statins, with no RCT ever run. He notes Ornish did run an RCT with results similar to the large Mediterranean-diet trials, and criticizes Esselstyn's stance on olive oil, nuts and white potatoes while calling the diet healthy.

  • Esselstyn patients were mostly on statins; no randomized trial of the diet exists
  • Ornish RCT results were good but no better than big Mediterranean-diet trials
  • Trials cited for the Mediterranean diet: Lyon Heart Study, PREDIMED, CORDIOPREV

I have nothing against the Esselstyn diet. I think it is a very healthy diet.

But the evidence that Esselstyn has published supporting it is nothing more than a collection of anecdotes. This is why most cardiologists won’t recommend it… even though they won’t outright discourage it either. Case-based observational studies like Esselstyn has published are at the bottom of the evidence hierarchy in medicine:

↪ Check where a study sits on the evidence hierarchy before citing it

All of Esselstyn’s patients in his published case-based observational studies were heavily medicated, and the vast majority were taking statins. Esselstyn himself has admitted that much of the benefit came from the medications, especially the statins. You can read it in his own words here:

↪ Esselstyn's reversal results paired a plant-based diet with lipid drugs, mostly statins

Esselstyn has never run a randomized controlled trial to test his diet properly.

Funding for such a trial is not an issue. His family has a thriving business promoting the diet that provides them with a nice stream of income. They could easily use some of that money to fund an RCT to test how well the diet really works. But they have repeatedly chosen not to. Ask yourself why? They have had no trouble raising money for documentaries like Forks over Knives and other projects to promote the diet. That money would have been much better spent by running a proper RCT. There are also rich and famous people on this diet that could easily help raise money for such an RCT (e.g. Bill Clinton) if Esselstyn really wanted to run one. He clearly does not.

At least Ornish did run a proper RCT. Ornish’s results were good, but no better than the big trials on the traditional Mediterranean diet:

↪ Ornish trial: lifestyle program cut cardiac events, but plant-based diet was not a cure

↪ Meta-analysis of 4 Mediterranean diet RCTs: MACE down 48%, CVD death down 46%

The Esselstyn diet is healthy, but it likely doesn’t work any better than a traditional Mediterranean diet in combination with optimal use of medication.

All you have to do is join the multiple Esselstyn groups on Facebook to see that many patients on the Esselsyn diet still see their heart disease progress, especially if they refuse medication.

Esselstyn cherry picks the best cases when he talks about his diet, and pretends that the failures don’t exist. Every time there is a failure, he claims that the patient was “non-compliant”. This just isn’t true. Some of the patients in these Facebook groups follow the Esselstyn diet religiously and still fail.

Esselstyn also promotes all kinds of pseudoscientific nonsense about olive oil and nuts/seeds, and promotes high consumption of white potatoes which many studies have shown likely cause heart disease and diabetes.

Virtually everyone in cardiology and academic nutrition thinks that what Esselstyn says about olive oil and nuts causing heart disease is nonsense— the best evidence shows the exact opposite of what he claims.

Even Ornish now recommends consumption of nuts and seeds because the evidence is so strong that they protect against heart disease.

The Ornish program to reverse heart disease now allows nut and seed consumption.

They changed their former ultra low fat position because of the strong evidence that nuts and seeds protect against CVD.

Quoted from the Ornish website:

“The Dr. Dean Ornish’s Program for Reversing Heart Disease now allows a limited amount of select nuts and seeds. The reason for this change is because in the last decade a lot of research has focused on the influence of eating nuts and seeds on our health. Both epidemiological studies and clinical trials have shown impressive evidence that nut consumption is associated with cardiovascular health and the reduction of major chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes.

Consuming nuts and seeds correlates with a consistent 30-50% lower risk of cardiovascular disease.

Several of the largest cohort studies, including the Adventist Study, the Iowa Women’s Health Study, the Nurses’ Health Study, and the Physicians’ Health Study have shown that consuming nuts and seeds correlates with a consistent 30-50% lower risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death, and stroke. Decreased cholesterol has also been associated with eating nuts.”

“The PREDIMED study, a 5 year randomized control study that was published in 2013 in the New England Journal of Medicine, tracked 7216 patients who were all at high risk of heart disease.”

“The most relevant findings after the five-year study was that the group that included nuts cut their incidence of stroke in half. The results also showed the increased frequency of nut consumption was associated with a reduced risk of mortality.”

“A 2015 meta-analysis of 61 controlled intervention trials also investigated the effects of nuts, including walnuts, almonds, pistachios, pecans, and cashews on cholesterol, blood pressure, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and inflammation (C-reactive protein). The study found that nut intake lowered total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides. Another pooled analysis published in JAMA in 2010, looked at 25 intervention studies examining the effects of nut consumption on cholesterol levels. The study concluded that nut consumption decreased blood cholesterol (LDL) levels.”

“There is also considerable research showing the benefits of nut consumption on the reduction of inflammation and LDL oxidation that leads to plaque development, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and other related metabolic disturbances and biomarkers for disease risk.”

Why a cross-sectional NHANES keto mortality study sits low on the evidence hierarchy

Louis Nervegna · Jan 24, 2025 · ♥ 8 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post
Low-carb, keto & carnivoreHow to read research
AI summary & key facts

Louis comments on a Scientific Reports NHANES study (43,776 adults, 2001-2018) suggesting a ketogenic diet may lower all-cause mortality without raising CVD mortality. He explains cross-sectional studies capture one snapshot and are only hypothesis-generating. Quality nutrition evidence comes from prospective cohorts and RCTs.

  • Study used 43,776 adults from NHANES 2001-2018
  • Cross-sectional studies are low on the evidence hierarchy
  • Prospective cohorts track people before disease; RCTs test causality directly

I’d like to comment on this recent observational keto study that is attracting attention on social media.

Study:

Paper · Sci Rep · 2024The ketogenic diet has the potential to decrease all-cause mortality without a concomitant increase in cardiovascular-related mortalitynature.com

This is what is called a cross-sectional study. It looks at only one snapshot in time whenever data is collected.

Cross-sectional studies are low on the evidence hierarchy in medicine. At best, they are used to generate hypotheses to be tested in higher quality studies. They are very cheap and easy to do, so they are good for generating hypotheses.

↪ Check where a study sits on the evidence hierarchy before citing it

Quality studies in nutrition are either prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials.

Prospective cohort studies are forward looking and track people continuously over time before they get diseases (not after). RCT’s directly test for causality.

The first question you should always ask yourself when evaluating a new study is where does it fall on the evidence hierarchy in medicine.

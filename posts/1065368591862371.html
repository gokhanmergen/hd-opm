Effects of Exercise on Atherosclerotic Plaque

Abstract

Regular exercise, particularly moderate-to-high intensity, has been shown to reduce coronary plaque burden and improve cardiovascular health in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). Studies indicate that both high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and moderate continuous training can lead to plaque regression, with HIIT potentially offering greater improvements in fitness and plaque reduction compared to usual care. Combining exercise with aggressive LDL-cholesterol control (targeting <70 mg/dL) yields additive benefits in reducing plaque volume, especially in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or those who have undergone stenting. While higher exercise intensity may be more beneficial for reducing the progression of coronary artery calcification (CAC), extremely high volumes or very vigorous exercise might paradoxically increase calcification, though often resulting in more stable, calcified plaques. Weight management through diet and exercise further enhances plaque regression and improves overall cardiometabolic health. Therefore, a balanced exercise program, individualized and combined with guideline-directed medical therapy, is crucial for maximizing plaque-stabilizing effects and improving cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CAD.

1. Exercise Training Lowers Atheroma Volume and Plaque Burden

High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) and Coronary Plaques

  • Vesterbekkmo et al. (2022, 2023)
  • Study design: Randomized controlled trials using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in patients with stable coronary artery disease (CAD) after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).
  • Intervention: 6 months of supervised HIIT twice weekly at 85%–95% of peak heart rate vs. usual care (contemporary preventive guidelines).
  • Key findings:
  • Plaque burden (PB): 1.4% greater reduction in PB in the HIIT group compared with control (p = 0.036).
  • Total atheroma volume (TAVnorm): Reduced by 9 mm³ in the HIIT group, with a between-group difference of 12 mm³ favoring HIIT (p = 0.003).
  • Cardiorespiratory fitness: VO₂peak improved by 1.7 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ more in the HIIT group vs. control (p = 0.039).
  • Conclusion: Supervised HIIT leads to regression of coronary plaque (both PB and TAV) and improves fitness in stable CAD patients.
  • Madssen et al. (2014)
  • Study design: 12-week randomized trial comparing aerobic interval training (AIT; ~90% HR_peak) vs. moderate continuous training (MCT; ~70% HR_peak) in patients post-stent implantation.
  • Key findings:
  • Both AIT and MCT groups showed significant reduction in necrotic core (−3.2% vs. −2.7%, respectively) and plaque burden (~−10.7%).
  • No significant differences between AIT vs. MCT in the degree of plaque regression—both were beneficial.
  • Conclusion: Even moderate or high-intensity aerobic training, when combined with optimal medical therapy, promotes plaque regression and reduces necrotic core.

Combined Exercise and Lipid-Lowering Therapy

  • Nishitani-Yokoyama et al. (2020)
  • Study design: Retrospective analysis of ACS (acute coronary syndrome) patients undergoing PCI, receiving both early-phase cardiac rehabilitation (exercise) and aggressive LDL-cholesterol control (<70 mg/dL).
  • Key findings:
  • Patients who both increased physical activity (≥7000 steps/day) and achieved LDL-C <70 mg/dL had greater plaque regression compared to those achieving only one or neither of these goals.
  • Conclusion: Combining exercise and optimal lipid-lowering (LDL-C <70 mg/dL) yields additive benefits in reducing coronary plaque volume.
  • Pedersen et al. (2019)
  • Study design: 1-year randomized trial comparing (a) aerobic interval training alone vs. (b) a low-energy diet + aerobic training in overweight patients with CAD.
  • Key findings:
  • Both interventions improved fitness, serum lipids, and inflammatory markers.
  • Diet + exercise led to superior weight loss and greater improvements in some plaque-related risk factors vs. exercise alone.
  • Conclusion: Dietary intervention combined with exercise is more effective than exercise alone for overall cardiometabolic health and potentially for plaque stabilization/regression.

2. Exercise Dose, Intensity, and Coronary Artery Calcification (CAC)

Higher Intensity vs. Higher Volume of Exercise

Several large observational studies have focused on coronary artery calcification (CAC) scores as a surrogate for coronary plaque burden and future cardiovascular risk.

  • Pavlovic et al. (2024)
  • Population: 23,383 apparently healthy men who underwent CAC scanning.
  • Findings:
  • Higher average intensity of physical activity → lower mean CAC and lower risk of having CAC ≥100 Agatston units (AU).
  • Longer duration (i.e., more total hours/week) → greater mean CAC and higher risk of having CAC ≥100 AU.
  • Interpretation: Emphasizing intensity over sheer volume may be more beneficial for reducing progression of CAC.
  • Aengevaeren et al. (2023) – MARC-2 Study
  • Population: 289 middle-aged/older male athletes followed for 6+ years.
  • Findings:
  • Total exercise volume was not associated with changes in CAC or plaque progression.
  • Vigorous intensity exercise was linked to less increase in CAC, whereas very vigorous exercise correlated with greater increase in CAC and calcified plaque.
  • Interpretation: Some very high-intensity exercise may paradoxically increase calcification, albeit often producing more stable (fully calcified) plaques.

Higher Volumes of Physical Activity and CAC Progression

  • Shuval et al. (2024)
  • Population: 8,771 healthy adults >40 years, mean follow-up 7.8 years.
  • Findings:
  • High and very high volumes of leisure-time physical activity were not associated with greater CAC progression.
  • Higher baseline physical activity did not translate into clinically meaningful CAC at follow-up.
  • Interpretation: Engaging in regular high-volume exercise does not inevitably lead to dangerous increases in plaque.

Plaque Composition: More Calcified vs. Non-Calcified Plaques

  • Aengevaeren et al. (2020) and Bosscher et al. (2023)
  • Key point: Athletes, especially those with very high lifetime training volumes, can show higher CAC but often with more calcified (stable) plaques and fewer mixed or vulnerable plaques.
  • Meaning: Calcified plaques are typically considered more stable and less prone to rupture than “soft” or mixed plaques. Thus, despite higher CAC scores, the overall risk of acute plaque rupture events may still be relatively low in these highly active individuals.

3. Practical Take-Home Messages for Patients

  1. Exercise Is Beneficial for Coronary Plaque Regression
  • Multiple IVUS studies (Vesterbekkmo, Madssen) show that 3–6 months of supervised exercise (particularly moderate-to-high intensity) can reduce plaque burden and necrotic core in patients with CAD, even after stenting.
  1. High-Intensity Training Can Be Especially Effective
  • HIIT at 85%–95% of peak heart rate has been shown to produce greater improvements in VO₂peak and more pronounced plaque regression than usual care (Vesterbekkmo et al.).
  • At the same time, moderate continuous training also helps (Madssen et al.), so individuals who cannot safely perform HIIT can still benefit from moderate exercise.
  1. Combine Exercise With Effective LDL-Cholesterol Control
  • Achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL (especially in patients with ACS or stents) is crucial.
  • Studies (Nishitani-Yokoyama) indicate that plaque regression is maximized by combining adequate physical activity (≥7000 steps/day) and aggressive LDL-lowering therapy.
  1. Mind Intensity vs. Volume
  • Higher-intensity exercise often provides greater cardiometabolic benefits and can be associated with less progression of CAC (Pavlovic, Aengevaeren).
  • Extremely prolonged or “very vigorous” sessions (for example, ultra-endurance) may slightly increase coronary calcification, but often this is the stable, calcified type of plaque rather than the more dangerous soft plaque.
  1. Weight Management Enhances Plaque Regression
  • In overweight CAD patients, diet + exercise results in greater weight loss, improved lipids, and further plaque regression (Pedersen et al.).
  • Maintaining a healthy body weight, controlling blood pressure, and optimizing lipid profile together have an additive effect.
  1. Quality Over Quantity
  • If you are medically cleared for exercise, focus on a balanced program that includes moderate to vigorous intensities, rather than just accumulating “hours” of low-intensity activity.
  • For many patients—especially those with stable CAD—the recommended 150+ minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity can be sufficient. For those who can tolerate it safely, adding intervals of vigorous exercise may bring extra benefits.
  1. Work With Your Healthcare Team
  • Always individualize exercise prescriptions based on overall clinical status (e.g., presence of stents, history of myocardial infarction, cardiopulmonary fitness, orthopedic issues).
  • Combine structured exercise with guideline-directed medical therapy (statins, blood pressure control, etc.) for maximum plaque-stabilizing effects.

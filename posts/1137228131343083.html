AI summary & key facts

Louis cites the Marklund 2019 pooled analysis of nearly 70,000 people in 30 prospective cohorts that measured omega-6 (linoleic acid) directly in blood and tissue. He argues the lack of any positive association, and in fact an inverse one, makes it virtually impossible that omega 6 causes heart disease or diabetes, and that biomarker studies are not food-questionnaire epidemiology.

  • Nearly 70,000 people from 30 prospective cohorts
  • Per interquintile range higher linoleic acid: 7% lower total CVD, 22% lower CVD mortality, 12% lower ischemic stroke
  • Biomarker-based, not food questionnaires

It is virtually impossible that omega 6 fats could cause heart disease or diabetes.

A positive association is a necessary requirement for causation. Two variables must— at bare minimum— be positively associated, if one actually causes the other.

Not only is there no positive association, but there is a very strong inverse association, as measured directly in blood and tissue over many years.

The lack of positive association makes causality near impossible for both heart disease and diabetes:

Measuring levels of omega 6 in blood and tissue and then tracking prospectively for many years is not “nutritional epidemiology”. That would be like calling the Framingham Heart Study nutritional epidemiology. There is no nutrition or diet questionnaires involved whatsoever. They periodically take blood samples and then track who gets heart attacks and who does not many years in the future. This is just like the way the Framingham Heart Study tracked cholesterol, except now the blood marker being tracked is omega 6 (linoleic acid).

Omega 6 polyunsaturated fats and HEART DISEASE

A pooled analysis of nearly 70,000 people from 30 prospective cohort studies showed that higher blood and tissue levels of omega 6 are associated with LOWER rates of heart disease.

Note that these studies were NOT based on food questionnaires. They were based on direct measurements of omega 6 levels in blood and tissue. And they tracked the omega 6 levels in the blood periodically for years before people developed heart disease (hence the use of the term prospective in describing the studies).

People who had omega 6 blood levels in the highest 90-percentile of the range had 22% less risk of eventually dying from CVD than people in the bottom 10-percentile of the range.

Quoted:

“Per interquintile range [90-percentile verses 10-percentile] higher LA levels were associated with 7% (95%CI, 1%–12%), 22% (15%–30%), and 12% (2%–21%) lower incidence of total CVD, CVD mortality, and ischemic stroke, respectively (Figures 2 and 3, Table 2).”

A shared post argues that human trial evidence does not support the claim that seed oils are harmful. Systematic reviews of randomized trials find that raising linoleic acid does not consistently increase inflammatory markers, replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers LDL and cuts coronary events, and trials show no significant increase in lipid peroxidation. It adds that LDL oxidation mostly occurs after retention in the artery wall, so LDL concentration still matters.

MESA: high Lp(a) mostly matters when CAC is 100 or above

Louis Nervegna · Jul 18, 2024 · ♥ 4 reactions · 1 comments · original Facebook post
Lp(a)Plaque, CAC & CT angiographyRisk assessment & lab tests
AI summary & key facts

A repost of a MESA analysis suggesting that people with high Lp(a), CAC under 100 and hsCRP under 2 mg/L have 10-year risk not much above those with normal Lp(a). The highest risk is with elevated Lp(a) and CAC of 100 or more. Louis notes this is a cohort study, not an RCT.

  • Highest risk: elevated Lp(a) plus CAC >= 100
  • CAC 1-99: Lp(a) high or low matters little over 10 years
  • Observational prospective cohort, not an RCT

Not everyone with high Lp(a) appears to be at higher 10 year risk.

The MESA study has shown that people with high Lp(a) but CAC<100 and hsCRP<2mg/L do not appear to be at much higher risk than people with normal Lp(a).

Keep in mind that the MESA study is a prospective cohort study and not a randomized controlled trial.

Analysis of data from the MESA study suggests that high Lp(a) levels are mainly a problem when CAC score exceeds 100.

Patients with high Lp(a) and CAC below 100 are at 10 year risk levels that are considerably lower.

The high Lp(a) does not seem to significantly increase 10 year risk of heart attack or stroke unless the CAC exceeds 100.

Quoted:

Both lipoprotein(a) and coronary artery calcium (CAC) are independently associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with the highest risk seen among people with elevated Lp(a) and CAC ≥ 100, a new analysis of the MESA study shows.

Quoted:

The findings confirm that at least over a decade, a “calcium score of zero sort of trumps the Lp(a) being high,” Joshi said.

However, he was not expecting to see the CAC score “tell the whole story” up to a score of 100 at least. “So if you have a calcium score that's between 1 and 99, the Lp(a) being high or low doesn't matter as much,” he said. “The risk is about the same, but where it really does change is above a hundred.

Louis summarizes a MESA cohort analysis in Progress in Cardiovascular Diseases showing waist-to-hip ratio (a visceral-fat and liver-fat proxy) modifies the ASCVD risk of Lp(a), while BMI did not. Lp(a) of 50 mg/dL or more with high WHR carried the excess risk, which mostly vanished when WHR was low.

Muscle aging involves falling chaperone-mediated autophagy; fasting and exercise raise it

Louis Nervegna · Dec 08, 2025 · ♥ 12 reactions · 7 comments · original Facebook post
Aging & longevityFasting & meal timingExercise & fitness
AI summary & key facts

Louis shares two Nature Metabolism papers from Ana Maria Cuervo's group showing chaperone-mediated autophagy declines with age in muscle and that restoring it improved strength and regeneration in aging mice. He notes fasting and resistance training increase CMA, rapamycin activates it and excess high-quality protein inhibits it, and ties it to heart muscle.

  • Sarcopenia affects half of people over 80
  • CMA decline weakens muscle repair; boosting it restored strength in aging mice
  • Exercise and fasting increase CMA
  • Louis: rapamycin activates, excess protein inhibits CMA

It is catabolic processes that keep our muscles healthy as we age. Our muscles need catabolic processes like autophagy to age in a healthy way.

The heart is a muscle.

Fasting naturally increases the chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) that protects muscle from age-related decline. Exercise like resistance training acts catabolically to increase CMA.

Note: The drug rapamycin activates CMA in muscle. Excessive high-quality protein inhibits CMA. See comments.

Quoted:

Sarcopenia or muscle wasting often accompanies aging, affecting half of all people over age 80 and significantly raising their risk for falls, injuries, and hospitalizations," said Dr. Cuervo, distinguished professor of developmental & molecular biology and of medicine, the Robert and Renée Belfer Chair for the Study of Neurodegenerative Diseases, and co-director of the Institute for Aging Research at Einstein.

"One of our interesting findings is that simple lifestyle interventions, such as exercise and fasting, can boost CMA activity and help prevent age-related muscle wasting."

The research, conducted in animal models and human muscle samples, found that CMA preserves muscle strength and enables muscles to repair themselves and that boosting CMA activity is a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing age-related muscle decline. Both studies were led in part by Albert Einstein College of Medicine's Ana Maria Cuervo, M.D, Ph.D., one of the world's leading authorities on the role of CMA in health and disease.

CMA allows cells to eliminate damaged or unnecessary proteins and reuse parts of them to build new proteins. In previous studies, Dr. Cuervo and colleagues demonstrated that reduced CMA activity allows intracellular "garbage" to build up in nerves and other cells, leading to neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's, as well as vascular and metabolic diseases. However, little was known about the role of CMA in skeletal muscle health.

Quoted:

CMA decline may be one of the earliest molecular events in muscle aging, said Dr. Cuervo. "As we age, a decline in CMA activity is noticeable in multiple organs, including muscle. We found this in the muscle from both our mouse models and in healthy, elderly people that we examined as part of this study—even before clear evidence that muscle function has deteriorated.

"We also found that the muscles of elderly patients with sarcopenia had markedly reduced CMA activity. Our findings identify CMA as a vital guardian of muscle integrity and a promising target for preventing or treating sarcopenia in aging populations."

Shared from Albert Einstein College of Medicine · original post

Why do our muscles weaken as we age? New research led in part by Ana Maria Cuervo, M.D., Ph.D., and published in Nature Metabolism, reveals a key piece of the puzzle.

Scientists discovered that a vital “clean-up” process inside our cells – chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) – helps keep muscles strong by removing damaged proteins and supporting muscle repair. As we get older, CMA slows down, leading to muscle weakness and age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia).
Researchers found that:

  • When CMA declines, muscles struggle to repair themselves
  • Boosting CMA restored strength and regeneration in aging mice
  • Poor diet reduces CMA activity
  • Exercise and fasting can naturally increase CMA

These findings position CMA as a powerful new target for preventing or treating age-related muscle decline. To learn more, click Age-Related Muscle Wasting Tied to Cell Recycling Defect (montefioreeinstein.org)

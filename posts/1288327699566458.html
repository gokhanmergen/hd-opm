Blending whole fruit does not cause bigger blood-sugar spikes than eating it whole

Louis Nervegna · May 10, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 13 comments · original Facebook post
Carbs, fiber, sugar & whole grainsInsulin resistance & diabetesMyths, misinformation & controversies
AI summary & key facts

Louis rebuts the claim that blending fruit spikes blood sugar. Per the shared response, an RCT comparing whole apples and blackberries with a smoothie of the same fruits found a slightly smaller glucose rise with the smoothie, and blending mangoes also did not raise the spike. Fiber remains in the blend and still slows sugar absorption.

  • RCT: smoothie gave slightly smaller glucose spike than whole apples and blackberries
  • Blending mango did not produce a larger spike
  • Fiber stays in the blend and slows sugar absorption

Blending whole fruit does NOT cause higher blood sugar spikes than eating whole fruit.

This is FALSE.

In fact, some studies actually show that blending whole fruit lowers the blood sugar spike, because it causes the soluble fiber in the fruit to bind more strongly with the sugar. This slows the absorption of the sugar into the bloodstream.

“Blending fruits is bad for blood sugar!” (RESPONSE)

What @garybrecka’s saying just isn’t true. Blending fruits has either little impact or can even reduce the glycemic index. For example, in one RCT, whole apples and blackberries were pitted against a smoothie made with those fruits and the smoothie actually resulted in a slightly smaller glucose “spike” than the whole fruits, similar to results from another trial that used different fruits. And even for somewhat higher glycemic index fruits like mangoes, blending does not result in a larger “spike.”

The reason we don’t see a larger “spike” is likely because the fibre is still there to slow the absorption of the sugar. So it’s not clear what he means when he says you “disperse” the fibre but it is clear that blending fruits doesn’t “quadruple” their impact on blood sugar.

Now, the reason that blending some fruits like blackberries may even lower the impact on blood glucose is because they’re rich in polyphenols and contain plenty of tiny seeds, which we can’t really access by just chewing. However, blending can break open those tiny seeds, releasing more fibre and phytochemicals.

But with all that out of the way, I want to clarify that there really isn’t any good evidence that people without diabetes need to be concerned about the short-term “spikes” in blood glucose after meals despite the common social media narratives, and the consumption of many fruits is associated with a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

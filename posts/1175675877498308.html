A 92-year-old athlete's fitness mixes exercise benefit with genetic reverse causality

Louis Nervegna · Dec 06, 2025 · ♥ 12 reactions · 1 comments · original Facebook post
How to read researchExercise & fitnessAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

Louis comments on a CNN story about a 92-year-old athlete, arguing her health is probably both caused by exercise and a reason she can exercise so hard. He notes many observational exercise studies are inflated by reverse causality and survivorship bias, and welcomes research separating the two.

  • Forward causality: exercise improves health
  • Reverse causality: good genetics and health allow intense exercise
  • Observational exercise studies are inflated by reverse causality
  • Studies of very old people suffer survivorship bias

Her case is almost certainly a combination of both forward and reverse causality.

The exercise is a big part of the reason why she is doing so well at 92. This is the forward causality.

But her genetics (and perhaps other aspects of her lifestyle) are likely a major reason why she is capable of exercising so intensely at 92yo. This is the reverse causality.

Sorting out the relative contributions of each of these directions of causality is one if the reasons why they are doing this study.

Unfortunately, many observational studies on exercise involve a large degree of reverse causality, which inflates their results:

↪ Finnish twin study suggests exercise's mortality benefit is partly reverse causality

Studies in people her age also involve a good deal of what statisticians call survivorship bias:

↪ Centenarians' higher LDL is survivorship bias, not evidence that high LDL is protective

↪ Victorian-diet longevity claim is survivorship bias from excluding deaths under age 5

Most people are not aware of this.

I’m glad scientists are now working to sort this out, so we can quantify precisely the true causal effect that exercise has on longevity in humans.

Victorian-diet longevity claim is survivorship bias from excluding deaths under age 5

Dec 01, 2023 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 6 ↩ 1 How to read research

Louis uses a paper on mid-Victorian diets as an example of survivorship bias. The authors exclude deaths before age 5, but those who survive early childhood were genetically selected for health, so their long lives do not show that the diet made average adults live as long as people today. Including the weaker people would have produced a much lower average age of death. The linked paper itself says life expectancy at age 5 was as good or better than today.

Finnish twin study suggests exercise's mortality benefit is partly reverse causality

Nov 01, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 2 ↩ 4 Exercise & fitness

Louis cites a Finnish twin study of 22,750 people suggesting that part of the observed link between physical activity and lower mortality may reflect healthier people exercising more. The authors concluded activity may reflect a healthy phenotype rather than causally lowering mortality.

Mechanical stress on bones made mice live nearly 3 months longer via anti-aging signals

Nov 03, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 6 Exercise & fitness

A news report on a mouse study found that mechanical strain on the skeleton, similar to what occurs in exercise and resistance training, makes osteocytes secrete anti-aging chemicals. Gently squeezing leg bones extended mouse lifespan by nearly 3 months, roughly nine human years. A related study found bone stress improved memory and motor function after traumatic brain injury in mice and pigs.

Exercise-trained older adults' muscle looked younger at the molecular level

Sep 18, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 1 Exercise & fitness

A Nature Aging multi-omics study compared skeletal muscle from younger and older adults and found that about half of the age-related molecular differences were absent in exercise-trained older adults, who looked more like young muscle. Gene expression for cellular respiration and energy metabolism was preserved.

Review of 350+ studies links protein restriction to better metabolism and healthier aging

Aug 05, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 5 ↩ 1 Protein

Dudley Lamming and colleague reviewed over 350 studies and report that lower protein intake is associated with improved metabolism and healthier aging via mechanisms like a liver hormone. Lamming says benefits of protein are clear for active people but sedentary people may overeat it.

Muscle biological age is the top cell-type predictor of mortality in 60,542 people

Jun 24, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 3 Aging & longevity

Louis shares a 2026 Nature Medicine study using plasma proteins from 60,542 people to estimate the biological age of over 40 cell types. Skeletal muscle aging best predicted all-cause mortality. He cautions about reverse causality but notes it offers a biological rationale for resistance training lowering mortality.

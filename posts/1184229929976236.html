Frailty in older adults is not a protein-deficiency disease, per Luigi Fontana

Louis Nervegna · Dec 19, 2025 · ♥ 14 reactions · 9 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis shares Luigi Fontana's argument that frailty and sarcopenia in Western older adults reflect decades of overnutrition and inactivity, not low protein. The post cites randomized trials in which protein supplements gave small or no functional gains, while resistance exercise produced the functional benefit.

  • PROVIDE trial (n=380, 13 weeks): no difference in handgrip or SPPB; +0.17 kg lean mass
  • Park et al., AJCN 2018 (n=120): only 1.5 g/kg beat 0.8 g/kg; 1.2 g/kg showed no difference
  • Tieland 2012 (n=62, 24 weeks): lean mass +1.3 vs -0.3 kg but functional gains came from resistance training

Frailty and sarcopenia: stop blaming “low protein”

In my clinical practice, I see older adults who are frail yet eat meat, milk, cheese, poultry, and eggs every day.
They are not protein-deficient. They are metabolically damaged after decades of over-nutrition and physical inactivity.

Yet we keep pushing the narrative that frailty is caused by “too little protein” and can be fixed with protein supplements and shakes.

The randomized trial data tell a very different story.
🔹 PROVIDE trial (n=380, 13 weeks, whey + leucine + vitamin D vs isocaloric control):
No difference in primary outcomes (handgrip strength, SPPB).
A small signal only: +0.17 kg appendicular lean mass and ~1 s faster chair-stand. That’s not a therapy.

🔹 Park et al., AJCN 2018 (n=120, 12 weeks, no exercise; 0.8 vs 1.2 vs 1.5 g/kg/day protein):
Only 1.5 g/kg/day differed from 0.8:
+0.52 kg vs +0.08 kg appendicular lean mass; gait speed +0.09 vs +0.04 m/s.
1.2 g/kg/day = no difference.

🔹 Tieland et al. 2012 (n=62, 24 weeks, resistance training ± protein):
Protein increased lean mass slightly (+1.3 kg vs −0.3 kg), but functional gains came from resistance exercise, not protein.

🔻 Uncomfortable conclusion:
In most Western older adults, sarcopenia is not a protein-deficiency disease.
It is a disease of lifelong metabolic overload, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and loss of regenerative capacity.

Adding protein late in life—without changing the biological terrain—is often just a bandage, not a solution.

If we want to change frailty trajectories, we need less focus on adding substrate and more focus on sinergistically reducing metabolic damage (by improving diet) and restoring mechanical signaling with resistance exercise.

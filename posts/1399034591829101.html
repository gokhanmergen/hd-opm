The fascinating history of CT scans.

Quoted:

The first CT scan found a brain tumor.

On 1 October 1971, at a small London hospital, a woman with a suspected brain tumor lay down inside a machine that existed nowhere else on Earth. It was built not by a medical company but by Godfrey Hounsfield, an engineer at the record label EMI, who had worked out that thin X-ray beams fired through the head from many angles could let a computer calculate what must be inside.

The scanner clicked around her head slice by slice for many minutes, and the machine could not even make the picture itself: the data was recorded and taken across town to a computer big enough to do the math. The image that came back was a crude grid of 80 by 80 blocks, blurry as a photograph made of dice, and in those blocks was a dark circle. The tumor, exactly where surgeons then found it. Radiologist James Ambrose knew at once what it meant. Hounsfield shared the 1979 Nobel Prize with physicist Allan Cormack, and CT scanners spread to every hospital on Earth.

Your hospital has one down the hall right now. Every CT scan ever made is a descendant of one woman's morning in 1971.

REACT: silent atherosclerosis found in 57% of 16,808 adults, 7-9% of 18-29-year-olds

Sep 26, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 11 · 💬 2 Plaque, CAC & CT angiography

The NEJM REACT study used carotid and femoral ultrasound plus coronary CT angiography in 16,808 adults aged 18-70 without known disease. Plaque was present in 57% overall, appeared in young adults, and a zero calcium score often missed early non-calcified plaque. Ten-year risk scores detected very little silent disease, so keeping risk factors favorable throughout life is the priority.

Same CT plaque, lower MI risk in women than men in a 10,003-person Danish cohort

Aug 30, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 3 Plaque, CAC & CT angiography

A Danish population study of 10,003 asymptomatic adults over 40 found subclinical coronary plaque less frequent in women at every age, for example 6% vs 20% at age 40-44. After 6 years, obstructive disease carried 4.1-fold higher MI risk in women versus 12-fold in men, though the sex interaction was not statistically significant.

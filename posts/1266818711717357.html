Vitamin C does not meaningfully raise iron levels in iron deficiency, meta-analysis finds

Louis Nervegna · Apr 12, 2026 · ♥ 4 reactions · 5 comments · original Facebook post
Supplements & vitamins
AI summary & key facts

A systematic review of 10 RCTs and 1 cohort (over 2,000 patients) found vitamin C given with oral iron had no clinically relevant effect on hemoglobin or ferritin. Louis notes excess iron may contribute to CVD and aging.

  • 10 randomized trials + 1 cohort, >2,000 patients
  • No clinically relevant change in hemoglobin or ferritin

Vitamin C does not increase iron absorption in a clinically meaningful way, according to a recent review and meta-analysis of human trials.

Excess iron is unhealthy, and may contribute to the development of several diseases, including CVD— as well as to the aging process itself.

It is commonly believed that vitamin C increases iron levels, when consumed along with iron-containing foods or supplements.

This recent review of 10 randomized trials and 1 cohort study involving over 2000 patients suggests that vitamin C (taken in this way) has no clinically relevant effect on iron levels.

The original review and meta-analysis can be found here:

Paper · Blood Vessel Thromb Hemost · 2024Efficacy of vitamin C with Fe supplementation in patients with iron deficiency anemia: a systematic review and meta-analysis.Oral iron (Fe) supplementation is one of the mainstays of treatment for iron deficiency anemia (IDA). However, its therapeutic effects are limited when there is poor absorption from the gastrointestinal tract. Vitamin C is hypothesized to improve uptake when combined as an adjunct agent. We aimed to determine the difference in hematologic outcomes in patients with IDA receiving oral iron, with or without vitamin C.…pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

The article in the post below is expert commentary on the meta-analysis published in NEJM Clinical. (See comments for a lengthy summary, if you are unable to get past the paywall.)

Note that a newly published study actually suggests that vitamin C protects against the pro-aging effects of iron in monkeys: facebook.com/share/p/1GUvj5yrFd.

Key findings of the review:

Adding Vitamin C to oral iron supplements does not provide clinically meaningful improvements in hemoglobin or ferritin levels for treating iron deficiency. While statistically significant, the increases in hemoglobin (0.14 g/dL Hb) and ferritin (3.23 ug/L) are too small to be clinically relevant.

  • Minimal Clinical Benefit: The addition of Vitamin C showed only a minimal, non-meaningful increase in iron levels.
  • Adverse Events: The overall incidence of side effects (e.g., gastrointestinal discomfort) did not differ between taking iron with or without Vitamin C.
  • Conclusion: The evidence suggests the common practice of co-administering Vitamin C with oral iron does not offer a significant advantage.

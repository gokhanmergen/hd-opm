Vitamin K2 slows coronary calcium mainly in severe calcification; event benefit unproven

Gokhan Can · Dec 02, 2025 · ♥ 18 reactions · 16 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Review of vitamin K2 (with or without vitamin D) and CAC. A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (n=1,533) found a -17.3 Agatston units/year pooled difference. The largest RCT (389 men, 720 ug MK-7 + 1000 IU D vs placebo for 2 years) missed significance overall but slowed CAC at baseline CAC >=400; some CKD trials were null. Not yet a standard therapy.

  • 2023 meta-analysis: 14 RCTs, n=1,533, annual CAC change -17.3 AU (95% CI -34.2 to -0.56, p=0.04)
  • 389 older men: CAC +203 vs +254 AU overall (p=0.089); CAC >=400 subgroup +288 vs +380 AU (p=0.047)

  • Smaller study: 360 ug/day MK-7 ~6% vs ~12% progression
  • BASIK2 trial (400 patients, Denmark) ongoing

Vitamin K2 (± Vitamin D) and Coronary Artery Calcification

Mechanism of Action Vitamin K2 is hypothesized to inhibit arterial calcium deposition by activating Matrix Gla Protein (MGP), a potent inhibitor of vascular calcification. This mechanistic link has driven research into using high-dose Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) to slow the progression of Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC).

Key Clinical Evidence

  • 2023 Meta-Analysis (14 RCTs, n=1,533):
  • Finding: Vitamin K supplementation demonstrated a statistically significant deceleration of CAC progression compared to placebo.
  • Data: The pooled mean difference in annual CAC change was –17.3 Agatston units (95% CI: –34.2 to –0.56; p = 0.04).
  • Safety: The safety profile was comparable to placebo.
  • The Hasifić et al. Trial (2023) – Largest RCT to Date:
  • Design: Randomized 389 older men with high vascular calcification to receive high-dose K2 (720 µg/day MK-7) + Vitamin D (1000 IU) vs. placebo for 2 years.
  • Overall Cohort Results: Both groups saw CAC increases (+203 AU for treatment vs. +254 AU for placebo). The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.089).
  • High-Risk Subgroup (Baseline CAC ≥400): Supplementation significantly slowed CAC growth compared to placebo (+288 AU vs. +380 AU; p = 0.047).
  • Clinical Outcomes: There were significantly fewer clinical events in the K2/Vitamin D arm (1.9% vs. 6.7%; p = 0.048), though the study was not powered to detect hard clinical endpoints.
  • Supporting & Conflicting Data:
  • Positive Signal: A smaller study on patients with moderate CAC (100–400) indicated that 360 µg/day of MK-7 slowed progression by roughly 6% over two years, compared to a 12% progression in the placebo group.
  • Null Results: Some trials, particularly within chronic kidney disease (CKD) populations, have shown no benefit regarding calcification scores.

Current Consensus & Future Outlook While Vitamin K2 is safe and biologically plausible, evidence remains mixed. It appears most effective in slowing progression among patients with severe existing calcification (CAC ≥400), mirroring findings seen in statin therapy.

It is not yet a standard recommended therapy for high CAC, but it remains an area of active investigation (e.g., the ongoing 400-patient BASIK2 trial in Denmark) to definitively determine if K2 retards arterial calcification.

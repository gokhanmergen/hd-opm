Meta-analysis of 119 trials: nuts did not cause weight gain and modestly lowered LDL

Louis Nervegna · Oct 29, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 2 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials (154 articles, 119 unique RCTs) found adding about 120 kcal/day of nuts did not significantly change weight, BMI or waist; body fat fell slightly and lean mass rose. Nuts were associated with small reductions in total cholesterol, LDL and triglycerides, with no significant effect on HDL.

In a systematic review of 119 randomized trials, adding 120 calories of nuts to participant’s diets did not result in weight gain. Fat was lost and lean mass increased. The changes were modest but real.

Nut consumption was also associated with small reductions in total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, with no significant effect on HDL cholesterol.

The systematic review covered 154 articles representing 119 unique randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and 102 were included in the further quantitative meta-analysis. The median follow-up was 21 weeks, with trial durations varying considerably. The articles reported results from both crossover and parallel-group trials.

Nut consumption was associated with an increase in energy intake of about 120 kcal/day, which the authors attributed to nuts being added to the usual diet in most trials. Overall, nut consumption did not significantly change body mass, body mass index (BMI), or waist circumference. The authors suggested that participants may have adjusted what they ate alongside nuts, but the trials did not establish why higher reported calorie intake did not produce measurable weight gain.

Overall, nut consumption reduced percentage fat mass by approximately 0.3 percentage points, and absolute fat mass by about 0.41 kg. It also raised percentage lean mass by 0.71 percentage points. Absolute lean mass did not change significantly, suggesting that the higher lean percentage reflected a shift in body proportions rather than demonstrating an actual gain in muscle mass.

The authors suggest that possible MUFA-related improvements in mitochondrial function and lower oxidative stress might help preserve muscle tissue. The trials did not directly establish these mechanisms or an increase in absolute muscle mass.

Compared with carbohydrate-rich controls, nuts reduced percentage fat mass by about 0.5 percentage points and fat mass by about 0.5 kg. In calorie-restricted trials, nuts were associated with greater lean mass (about 0.97 kg), greater lean percentage and lower fat percentage, together with a reduction in absolute fat mass of about 0.51 kg. The authors tentatively attribute this to nut protein helping preserve muscle during restriction, but a 2024 meta-analysis did not confirm the lean-mass finding.

The findings showed that nut consumption was associated with small reductions in total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, with no significant effect on HDL cholesterol.

Both nut types lowered total and LDL cholesterol, consistent with earlier research. Only the MUFA-rich subgroup showed a significant reduction in triglycerides, but the evidence for this outcome was very uncertain. The authors suggest that this could be because most trials with carbohydrate-rich controls included MUFA-rich nuts, limiting comparisons between groups. Dietary research predicts that replacing some dietary carbohydrates with fatty acids lowers triglyceride levels.

The researchers offer the following plausible mechanisms for these actions. Both PUFA and MUFA may reduce cholesterol synthesis and increase LDL cholesterol removal from the blood. MUFA could also affect the production and breakdown of triglyceride-rich particles. These pathways were proposed as explanations rather than directly tested in the pooled trials.

The study found no significant change in HDL-C levels after nut consumption, which contrasts with previous findings. The authors note that MUFA and PUFA intake from nuts was modest in this study, perhaps too low to affect the complex regulation of HDL cholesterol.

Conclusion: Nut consumption modestly reduced fat mass and improved body composition without changing body weight. It also improved total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, and MUFA-rich nuts were linked to lower triglycerides, though evidence for this outcome was very uncertain. The effect sizes are small and vary with comparator diet, while the fat-mass findings carry moderate certainty. The review did not establish whether these changes lead to meaningful long-term health benefits.

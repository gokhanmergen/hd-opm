ESC scientific statement: diet is a primary factor in CVD prevention and treatment

Louis Nervegna · Dec 01, 2025 · ♥ 22 reactions · 23 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis shares the European Society of Cardiology/EAPC scientific statement on diet and cardiovascular disease. It concludes that diet is a primary factor, that plant-based whole-food patterns lower risk, and that ultraprocessed food, processed and red meat, salt, sugar and saturated fat raise it.

  • Plant-based, whole-food patterns decrease risk
  • Ultraprocessed foods, processed meat, red meat, salt, sugar, saturated fat increase risk
  • Mediterranean and DASH diets have the best evidence

The European Society of Cardiology has released a scientific statement on the role that diet plays in the prevention and treatment of CVD:

Paper · Eur J Preventive CardiologyEur J Preventive Cardiology articleacademic.oup.com

Their intent is to summarize the preponderance of scientific evidence on this subject for practicing physicians.

Summary:

  • Diet plays a major role in prevention and treament of CVD. It is not just a secondary factor. It is a PRIMARY factor.
  • Plant-based dietary patterns rich in whole unprocessed foods decrease risk.
  • Diets high in ultraprocessed foods (including processed meat), red meat, salt, sugar, and saturated fat increase risk.
  • The traditional Mediterranean diet, DASH diet, and vegetarian diet reduce risk. The preponderance of evidence does not indicate that a pure vegan diet offers any additional benefit beyond this.
  • Low carb diets might be beneficial, but their long term effects have not been properly studied.
  • Controlling calories to stay in energy balance reduces risk.
  • Fasting can reduce CVD risk.
  • The consumption of 1/2 serving of alcohol per day (one unit) may reduce CVD risk, without presenting an unfavorable risk profile.

(Note: 14g of pure alcohol is considered a full serving. This is about 12oz of beer, 5oz of wine, or 1.5oz of distilled spirits.)

  • Moderate intake of coffee is safe and does not raise risk for CVD. It might be beneficial.
  • High potassium intake is beneficial. High sodium is detrimental.
  • In general, there is not convincing evidence that vitamin/mineral supplements protect against CVD.

Quoted:

“Plant-based dietary patterns rich in minimally processed foods, vegetables, and fruits reduce CVD risk, while patterns rich in ultra-processed foods, [red] meat, salt, sugar, and saturated fat increase risk. The Mediterranean, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension [DASH], and vegetarian diets reduce CVD risk, while vegan diets offer no additional benefit. Low-carbohydrate diets may be beneficial, but their long-term effect remains to be confirmed. Balanced distribution of caloric intake through different meals is associated with favourable effects. Fasting (e.g. alternate-day, intermittent, time-restricted, and periodic) can reduce CVD risk but is often challenging. Moderate coffee consumption is not associated with increased risk of CVD. The consumption of one unit of alcohol/day [1/2 serving] may contribute to a reduced cardiovascular risk without presenting an unfavourable risk profile. Generally, there is no evidence that vitamin and mineral supplementation reduces CVD risk. High potassium intake is beneficial in healthy individuals, calcium or selenium supplementation shows no added benefit, and high sodium intake is detrimental. Diet is a major component of CVD prevention, and health professionals should include dietary assessment and evidence-based recommendations in their clinical practice.”

Shared from Vital Human- Dr. Daniel Chong · original post

A major new scientific statement from the European Association of Preventive Cardiology and the European Society of Cardiology was just released, giving a thorough summary on how diet influences cardiovascular disease. To me, the with this paper is that they make it very clear that diet/nutrition is not just a minor or secondary factor. It's one of if not the most powerful, modifiable drivers of heart-disease risk across populations.

In the paper they clarify again the eating patterns with the most consistent and best evidence for heart-disease prevention are the Mediterranean diet, the DASH diet, and a whole-food, minimally processed, predominantly plant-based diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, and healthy sources of fat. These eating approaches show time and again reductions in LDL-related risk, inflammation, blood pressure, endothelial dysfunction, and overall cardiovascular events. I will add that I think which approach is best, and what ratio of macronutrients is best, is highly dependent on the individual, and people should strive to remain flexible in trying things and seeing how their bodies respond objectively, with a goal of optimal lipids, inflammation markers, and metabolic health markers.

The paper also makes it clear people wanting to avoid cardiovascular disease should eat less ultra-processed foods, red and processed meat, salt, refined sugar, and saturated fat intake. All of these dietary components consistently track with higher rates of hypertension, obesity, high LDL/apoB, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular mortality. The paper also emphasizes that extreme or highly exclusionary dietary approaches are less well proven long term.

Another crucial factor noted, that not enough people talk about, is that potassium-rich foods are especially important (ideally combined with lower sodium intake) and combined with overall high plant-food intake shows beneficial effects for blood pressure and vascular health, whereas vitamin and mineral supplements, on the whole, show no convincing evidence for preventing cardiovascular disease. In my opinion, it should be noted here that nutrition supplement studies often use low quality products or insufficient doses, and that should be taken into account in their assessment. Nonetheless, I agree with the authors in emphasizing that nutrients work best in their whole-food context rather than in isolated pill form, with the key being eating well enough, often enough to see those effects. That said, I see no reason not to combine things with a healthy diet as the foundation, adding supplements and meds as needed to get the best possible result.

This paper makes a great argument that nutrition deserves a clearer and much more prominent place amongst the tools we use to prevent heart attacks and strokes.

Magnesium: intake targets, widespread inadequacy and health roles (FoundMyFitness summary)

Gokhan Can · Mar 19, 2024 · ♥ 20 reactions · 3 comments · original Facebook post
Supplements & vitamins
AI summary & key facts

Summarizes a FoundMyFitness episode on magnesium, stressing that many people do not get enough and that deficiency can have wide-ranging health effects. It gives recommended intakes and argues that the RDA is a minimum to avoid deficiency disease.

  • Nearly 45% of the US population has inadequate magnesium intake
  • RDA: women 310-320 mg/day, men 400-420 mg/day
  • Athletes may need an extra 10-20% due to losses through sweat and urine

Introduction

  • Magnesium is a critical yet often overlooked mineral that plays a foundational role in many aspects of human health.
  • Nearly 45% of the US population has inadequate magnesium intake, primarily due to diets lacking in magnesium-rich foods like dark leafy greens.
  • Magnesium deficiency can have far-reaching negative health effects. Increasing magnesium intake through diet and supplementation may be key for optimal health.

Recommended Intake

  • Adult women need 310-320 mg magnesium per day (360 mg if pregnant). Adult men need 400-420 mg per day.
  • Athletes and highly active individuals may need an additional 10-20% above the RDA due to greater losses through sweat and urine.
  • The average intake in the US falls below the RDA, especially for women.
  • The RDA is the minimum amount to avoid severe deficiency diseases. Even meeting the RDA may mean only getting the bare minimum to function.

Magnesium Absorption and Bioavailability

  • Magnesium absorption from the diet is around 50%. It's lower with high fiber diets.
  • Cooking, heating, sprouting and fermentation can increase magnesium bioavailability from plant foods by activating phytase enzymes that break down phytates.
  • Excessive supplemental zinc (over 142 mg/day) can inhibit magnesium absorption. Brief high dose zinc during illness onset is an exception.

Reasons for Widespread Magnesium Insufficiency

  1. The standard American diet is calorically dense but low in magnesium-rich foods.
  2. Magnesium bioavailability from plants is limited unless techniques are used to reduce phytates.
  3. Health conditions like diabetes and alcohol consumption increase magnesium excretion.

Assessing Magnesium Status

  • Dietary tracking of magnesium intake is the most practical way to ensure adequate intake. The USDA Food Data Central database is a good resource.
  • Plasma and red blood cell magnesium tests are poor indicators of magnesium status in healthy people. They are more likely to identify severe deficiency.
  • Over time, the body pulls magnesium from bones to maintain blood levels, so blood tests can appear normal while bones become depleted.

Magnesium Supplements

  • The upper safe limit for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg/day. Exceeding this may cause GI issues like diarrhea.
  • Smaller doses spread throughout the day allow for better absorption than a single large dose.
  • Organic magnesium salts like magnesium glycinate, citrate, and malate are more bioavailable than inorganic forms like magnesium oxide.
  • Magnesium threonate is thought to cross the blood-brain barrier better, but should not count towards the RDA as it is very low in elemental magnesium.
  • The author takes a supplement with mixed magnesium salts to get benefits of different forms.

Stress and Magnesium

  • Stress triggers release of hormones like adrenaline and cortisol that can deplete magnesium levels.
  • Studies show both acute and chronic stress, even from things like sleep deprivation and test-taking, can significantly lower magnesium levels.
  • During the fight-or-flight response, magnesium is mobilized for immediate energy production. Prolonged stress gradually depletes the body's magnesium stores.

Exercise and Magnesium

  • Short intense exercise causes a temporary spike in blood magnesium to aid energy production.
  • Longer workouts over an hour deplete magnesium without this protective spike. Magnesium levels fall below baseline.
  • The increased energy demands of exercise affect the body's magnesium balance. Athletes require higher magnesium intake.

Magnesium and Sleep

  • Observational studies link higher magnesium intake to better sleep, but this could reflect an overall healthier lifestyle.
  • Randomized trials on magnesium supplementation and sleep show mixed results, with some benefits but many seeing no clear effect.
  • Nutrition trials are often flawed by not measuring baseline nutrient status. Giving magnesium to someone who already has sufficient levels is very different than correcting a deficiency.

Magnesium and Brain Health

  • An observational UK Biobank study of over 6000 people found higher magnesium intake correlated with greater gray matter volume in the brain, indicating slower brain aging.
  • This effect was most pronounced in women, with the high intake group averaging 550 mg magnesium per day compared to 350 mg in the lower group.
  • Though limited by the observational design, this suggests a possible neuroprotective effect of higher magnesium intake, especially for women.

Magnesium and Dementia/Alzheimer's

  • Alzheimer's patients have lower plasma and possibly brain magnesium levels compared to healthy controls.
  • Long-term observational studies link higher magnesium intake to lower dementia risk later in life.
  • Magnesium is a co-factor for key enzymes involved in cellular energy production, neurotransmitter synthesis, and preventing excitotoxicity in the brain.
  • Though not definitive proof, the evidence points to a potential role for magnesium in supporting brain health and cognitive function with aging.

Magnesium and Migraines

  • Many migraine sufferers are found to have magnesium deficiency or insufficiency.
  • Magnesium may help prevent cortical spreading depression (the brain signaling disruption linked to migraines), reduce pain-transmitting chemicals like substance P and glutamate, and prevent constriction of brain blood vessels.
  • A dose of around 600 mg magnesium per day, divided into smaller doses, was found in several small clinical trials to significantly reduce migraine frequency and severity.
  • While more research is needed, magnesium shows promise as a potential migraine management tool, especially for those with insufficient magnesium intake.

The Triage Theory, DNA Damage and Aging

  • Dr. Bruce Ames' triage theory posits that when nutrients are scarce, the body "triages" them to prioritize short-term survival and reproduction over long-term health.
  • Magnesium is directed to acute functions like energy production and away from long-term maintenance like DNA repair.
  • Though this doesn't cause immediate harm, over a lifetime this could allow DNA damage to accumulate, contributing to aging, mutations, and chronic diseases like cancer.
  • Subtle inadequacies in magnesium and other nutrients may thus have an insidious impact on the aging process and disease risk over time.

Magnesium and Reduced Cancer Risk

  • Magnesium is essential for genomic stability and DNA repair, replication and transcription. Deficiency allows more DNA damage and errors to occur.
  • Observational studies link low magnesium intake to significantly higher risk of pancreatic and colorectal cancers. Every 100 mg decrease in intake was tied to a 24% increase in pancreatic cancer rates.
  • Another study found the highest magnesium group had 40% lower all-cause mortality and 50% lower cancer mortality risk compared to the lowest group.
  • A meta-analysis calculated that each 100 mg increase in magnesium intake lowered all-cause mortality by 10% and cancer mortality by 5% in a dose-dependent manner.
  • Though only observational, the consistency of the evidence points to higher magnesium intake likely being protective against DNA damage and cancer.

Magnesium and Bone Health

  • Around 60% of the body's magnesium is stored in bone, where it supports skeletal structure and acts as a mineral reserve.
  • When intake is low, magnesium is pulled from bone to maintain blood levels. Over a lifetime, bones can lose nearly half their magnesium content.
  • Magnesium-rich diets in childhood support greater peak bone mass in young adulthood. One study found a year of magnesium supplementation increased bone mineral density in teen girls.
  • Getting adequate magnesium, especially earlier in life, may help prevent the bone loss and increased osteoporosis risk that comes with long-term insufficient intake.

Magnesium and Vitamin D

  • Magnesium acts as a cofactor for enzymes that metabolize vitamin D into its active forms. Deficiency impairs vitamin D metabolism.
  • Higher magnesium intake is associated with lower risk of vitamin D deficiency. Magnesium supplementation can increase blood levels of 25(OH)D, the main marker of vitamin D status.
  • Conversely, low magnesium intake hinders the body's ability to properly utilize vitamin D, even when vitamin D intake is sufficient.
  • The synergistic relationship between magnesium and vitamin D has implications for bone health and other areas influenced by vitamin D status.

Magnesium and Blood Pressure

  • Magnesium helps regulate blood pressure by increasing production of vasodilators like nitric oxide, fighting inflammation, and protecting blood vessels.
  • A meta-analysis of 34 controlled trials with over 2000 participants found magnesium supplementation at a mean dose of 368 mg/day significantly lowered both systolic and diastolic blood pressure.
  • The effect was dose-dependent and more pronounced with longer treatment duration. For some, magnesium may be a useful adjunct to other lifestyle and medical treatments for hypertension.

Magnesium and Muscle Cramps

  • Magnesium supports muscle relaxation and contraction. Deficiency may increase muscle excitability and cramping, making supplementation of interest for those prone to cramps.
  • However, evidence for magnesium reducing muscle cramps is mixed. Benefits are more often seen in pregnant women. Not all cramps are caused by low magnesium.
  • Epsom salt baths are popular for cramps, but it's scientifically unclear if meaningful amounts of magnesium can be absorbed through the skin to affect muscle tissue.
  • Magnesium may help some with muscle cramps, but the effect likely depends on the individual's magnesium status and the underlying cause of their cramps.

Conclusions

  • Magnesium is a critical nutrient with far-reaching effects on metabolic, cardiovascular, skeletal, neurological and muscle health.
  • Deficiency and insufficiency are common, and even mild inadequacies over time may raise the risk of chronic diseases and accelerate aging.
  • Increasing magnesium intake, preferably from nutrient-dense foods but also supplementation in some cases, may have profound benefits for health and longevity.
  • However, the effects may be most apparent in those who are deficient, with less noticeable impact expected for people whose magnesium intake is already sufficient.
  • An individualized approach considering dietary intake, health status, and personal response is ideal.
  • Though much is still to be uncovered, magnesium proves to be an often underappreciated but vitally essential piece of the nutritional puzzle for lifelong health and wellbeing.

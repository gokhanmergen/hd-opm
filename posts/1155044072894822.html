Senescent cells may drive atherosclerosis, making senolytics a possible target

AI summary & key facts

Louis suggests that senescent cells, which accumulate with age, are a causal factor in atherosclerosis, especially in the endothelium, and that drugs targeting them might help. The shared AFAR post notes mouse studies show senescent cells can be removed.

  • Senescent cells stop dividing but do not die and secrete harmful molecules
  • Mouse studies show they can be reduced

Senescent cells are a causal factor in atherosclerosis, particularly in the delicate endothelium. The older you get, the more of your cells ultimately become senescent. Drugs (or supplements) that target these cells might prevent and treat heart disease.

View from AFAR: “Zombie” Cells and Healthy Aging

Halloween may be over, but the “zombie” cells hiding in your body are here year-round!

In scientific terms, these are senescent cells – cells that have stopped dividing but don’t die. As we age, they can accumulate in tissues and secrete harmful molecules that degrade surrounding tissue, contributing to the decline of organ function.

While once controversial, research now shows that senescent cells play a damaging role in aging, but the good news is that they can be removed. Studies in mice have shown that it was possible to reduce the burden of senescent cells – extending not just lifespan, but healthspan.

Steven N. Austad, PhD, AFAR Scientific Director, explains:

"These 'zombie' cells are like a ticking time bomb in our tissues. They’ve lost their ability to perform their normal function, and they now can damage surrounding cells. The exciting part is that we now have strategies to selectively remove them, offering a potential path to healthier aging."

AFAR Grantee and Board Member James L. Kirkland, MD, PhD, has been a trailblazer in advancing senolytic therapies to remove these cells. Many other AFAR grantees are building on this foundational research.

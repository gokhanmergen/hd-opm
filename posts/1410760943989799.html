CoQ10 rejuvenated aging platelet-producing cells in mice via COPS3 and autophagy

Louis Nervegna · Oct 02, 2026 · ♥ 11 reactions · 0 comments · original Facebook post
Supplements & vitaminsHeart attack, stroke & proceduresAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

A mouse study in Aging Cell found dietary CoQ10 restored function of aged megakaryocytes, the bone marrow cells that make platelets, through a COPS3-autophagy pathway. Aging makes platelets stickier and more clot-prone, raising heart attack and stroke risk. The poster calls it promising but it is animal-only.

  • Dietary CoQ10 improved age-related megakaryocyte dysfunction in aged mice
  • Mechanism: COPS3-mediated activation of autophagy
  • Aging produces overly sticky, clot-prone platelets linked to heart attack and stroke

CoQ10 might protect against the aging-related changes in bone marrow which lead to the production of dysfunctional platelets as we get older.

These dysfunctional platelets are overly sticky and prone to clot. They increase risk of heart attack and stroke as people age.

This is a mouse study, but it looks very promising.

Quoted:

Researchers report that dietary CoQ10 can rejuvenate a critical population of bone marrow cells called megakaryocytes, the giant polyploid cells that manufacture every platelet circulating in the blood. The work identifies a previously unrecognized molecular circuit, running through a protein called COPS3 and the cellular waste-disposal system known as autophagy, that appears to explain how the supplement counteracts one of the more consequential consequences of growing old: platelets that become progressively stickier and more dangerous.

As people age, their platelets become hyperreactive, aggregating more readily and contributing to the elevated risk of heart attacks and strokes in older adults. Megakaryocytes sit at the origin of that problem. These enormous cells, which can contain up to 64 copies of the genome, undergo a metabolically demanding maturation program before shedding platelets into the bloodstream. Aging impairs their proliferation, differentiation, and quality-control machinery through both intrinsic cellular defects and changes in the bone marrow environment. Yet the precise molecular drivers of megakaryocyte decline during natural aging have remained poorly defined, and no targeted nutritional intervention had been shown to reverse it.

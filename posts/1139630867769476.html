Cutting dietary isoleucine by two-thirds extended male mouse lifespan 33%

Louis shares a Cell Metabolism mouse study in which restricting a single essential amino acid, isoleucine, lengthened life (33% in males, 7% in females), improved leanness, blood sugar, strength and frailty, and reduced age-related cancer. He notes the male effect matches major calorie restriction in mice, and suggests getting protein mainly from whole plant foods, which are naturally lower in isoleucine than animal protein. Shared from Daily Knowledge · original post

Cutting back on one specific amino acid slows the effects of aging and extends life, study shows.

The amino acid in question is isoleucine, an essential nutrient our bodies need but can’t produce on their own.

We get it from foods like meat, dairy, eggs, and soy. In this study, scientists from the University of Wisconsin tested how reducing isoleucine in the diet affected aging in mice. Some mice were fed a normal diet, others had all amino acids reduced, and a third group had only isoleucine cut by two-thirds. All groups could eat as much as they wanted.

The results were striking: male mice on the low-isoleucine diet lived 33% longer, while females lived 7% longer. These mice also had better health overall, they were leaner, had more stable blood sugar levels, better muscle strength, less frailty, and even less age-related cancer.

Oddly enough, they ate more than the other mice but still stayed lean, suggesting their bodies burned more energy. While these results are exciting, the researchers stress that it’s not as simple as telling people to eat less protein. Diets are complex, and isoleucine is only one of many essential nutrients.

Cutting protein too much can cause health problems, and the right balance likely varies between individuals. Still, the study points to the possibility that reducing or blocking just one amino acid could offer anti-aging benefits without completely overhauling your entire diet. The researchers hope that in the future, we might be able to develop drugs that mimic this effect in humans, targeting isoleucine specifically.

Fasting reported to target visceral and liver fat first, improving insulin resistance

Louis shares a BoxLife Magazine interview with Dr. Alan Goldhamer, who says supervised extended fasting preferentially mobilizes visceral fat, which kept declining during refeeding. This is claimed by Louis to explain fasting's effect on insulin resistance; the source is a magazine interview, not a peer-reviewed paper.

  • Visceral fat reportedly kept declining during refeeding and beyond
  • Claimed improvements in blood pressure and insulin sensitivity

Fasting attacks visceral fat first, particularly liver fat.

This is why it is so effective at reversing insulin resistance.

Quoted:

Dr. Goldhamer explained there are two types of body fat: subcutaneous (under the skin) and visceral (around organs). Visceral fat is metabolically dangerous, linked to inflammation and diseases of dietary excess.

The data revealed something extraordinary: visceral fat not only decreased during fasting but continued declining during refeeding and beyond. The body appeared to preferentially target this harmful fat for elimination.

Dr. Goldhamer described how the body demonstrates remarkable selectivity during fasting, protecting essential tissues like the nervous system while aggressively mobilizing pathological fat deposits.

Shared from BoxLife Magazine · original post

Dr. Alan Goldhamer argues that extended fasting does far more than simply reduce body weight—it appears to trigger a metabolic reset that preferentially targets dangerous visceral fat while preserving long-term improvements in body composition.

Drawing from thousands of medically supervised fasts and published clinical data, he explains how patients lost significant amounts of body fat, continued reducing visceral fat during refeeding, and often regained lean mass while maintaining their weight loss months later.

Beyond body composition, fasting produced striking improvements in blood pressure, insulin sensitivity, hormonal function, and autonomic nervous system balance, with some patients experiencing changes that persisted long after the fast ended.

The article also explores fasting's unexpected effects on taste preferences, food cravings, inflammation, and metabolic health, while outlining who may benefit, the potential risks, and why proper medical supervision remains essential for extended fasting protocols.

The full article dives into the clinical data, biological mechanisms, and practical insights behind what some researchers describe as one of the most powerful metabolic interventions available.

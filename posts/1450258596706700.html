EBV likely causes multiple sclerosis and may contribute to other autoimmune diseases

Shared from Genuinely Healthy · original post

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is one of the most common viruses in the world, infecting more than 90% of people during their lifetime. Best known for causing infectious mononucleosis, EBV can remain dormant in the body for decades, particularly inside immune cells called B cells. But researchers are discovering that its influence may extend far beyond a temporary infection.

In a 2018 study published in Nature Genetics, scientists at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center identified a potential connection between EBV and seven serious diseases: lupus, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, and type 1 diabetes.

Led by Dr. John Harley and colleagues, the research revealed that a viral protein called EBNA2 interacts with regions of human DNA associated with increased susceptibility to these conditions. Remarkably, EBNA2 was found at nearly half of the genetic risk regions linked to lupus, with similar associations identified across the other six diseases.

These findings suggest that EBV may influence autoimmune disease development by altering gene regulation in genetically susceptible individuals, although the study did not establish that the virus directly causes all seven conditions.

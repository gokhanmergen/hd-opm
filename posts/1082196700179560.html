Swapping 1 tbsp butter daily for plant oil linked to 17% lower cancer and all-cause death

Louis Nervegna · Aug 04, 2025 · ♥ 23 reactions · 20 comments · original Facebook post
Fats & oils (saturated fat, seed oils, omega-6, olive oil)CancerHeart attack, stroke & procedures
AI summary & key facts

Louis summarises a Harvard cohort analysis of over 200,000 adults across the Nurses' Health Study and related cohorts. Replacing butter with unsaturated plant oils was associated with lower cancer and overall mortality, with the biggest benefits from olive, canola and soybean oil.

  • Replacing 1 tbsp/day of butter with plant oil: 17% lower risk of cancer death and all-cause death
  • Each extra tbsp of butter/day: 12% higher cancer risk over 30 years
  • Each tbsp of plant oil: 11% lower cancer death, 6% lower CVD death
  • More than 200,000 US adults over 30+ years

The type fat you eat is not just influencing your risk for developing heart disease and diabetes.

In the long term (30 years), it is likely influencing your risk for developing cancer as well.

This study genuinely shocked me. What was so surprising was the large magnitude of the effect.

Shared from Louis Nervegna · original post

Huge high-quality prospective cohort study, just published today.

The research is from Harvard Medical School and Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston.

This is today’s press release from the American Heart Association.

Summary:

Replacing just one tablespoon of butter each day with any unsaturated plant oil is associated with a 17% reduction in risk from cancer and death from any cause over a 30 year time period.

Each additional tablespoon of butter consumed per day was associated with an additional 12% increased risk of cancer, over 30 years.

Each additional tablespoon of any unsaturated plant-based oil was associated with an 11% reduction in the risk of death from cancer and a 6% reduction in the risk of death from cardiovascular disease, over 30 years.

The biggest benefits came from olive, canola, and soybean oil.

Quoted:

“The study analyzed more than 3 decades of data for more than 200,000 adults in the U.S. and suggests that “even a modest daily change from butter to plant-based oils may lead to significant long-term health benefits,” the research authors noted.”

“The three cohorts analyzed by the researchers were the Nurses’ Health Study, the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study and the Nurses’ Health Study II. Researchers selected 221,054 participants not diagnosed with any major chronic diseases (cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, neurodegenerative diseases) at enrollment. Approximately 84% of participants self-identified as women and 16% self-identified as men; approximately 95% of total participants self-identified as non-Hispanic white adults.”

“Participants who replaced 10 gm (almost a tablespoon) of total butter intake each day with an equivalent amount of total plant-based oils had a 17% reduction in risk of death from all causes and a 17% reduction in risk of death from cancer.”

“Participants' consumption of butter, as well as their consumption of commonly used plant-based oils — safflower oil, soybean oil, corn oil, canola oil and olive oil — were examined.”

“Participants in the group with the highest daily butter intake had a 15% higher risk of all-cause death compared to those with the lowest butter intake. Each additional 10 grams per day of butter (just under one tablespoon) was associated with a 12% increased risk of death from cancer.”

“Those with the highest daily intake of plant-based oils had a 16% lower total risk of premature death. Every additional 10 grams daily of plant-based oils was associated with an 11% reduction in the risk of death from cancer and a 6% reduction in the risk of death from cardiovascular disease.”

“Our study found that higher butter intake was associated with increased deaths from all causes and cancer, while higher intake of plant-based oils was associated with lower deaths from all causes, cancer and cardiovascular disease,” said lead study author Yu Zhang, M.B.B.S., a graduate student at Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and a research assistant at Brigham and Women’s Hospital, both in Boston.”

Conventional produce beats no produce: natural plant carcinogens dwarf pesticide residues

Louis Nervegna · May 09, 2026 · ♥ 17 reactions · 10 comments · original Facebook post
Nuts, fish, coffee, tea, cocoa & other foodsCancerEnvironment (microplastics, pollution, heat)
AI summary & key facts

Louis argues that, while organic is fine if affordable, eating conventionally grown fruit and vegetables is much better than skipping them. He cites Ames's PNAS papers showing 99.99% by weight of dietary pesticides are plants' own natural defense chemicals, and that natural carcinogens in produce far exceed synthetic residues.

  • 99.99% of pesticides in the American diet by weight are natural plant defense chemicals (Ames 1990)
  • Natural carcinogens in produce occur in parts per 10,000-100,000

I am NOT arguing against organic food or banning dangerous synthetic pesticides.

I am in favor of all of this.

BUT you are FAR better off eating conventionally grown fruits and vegetables than not eating fruits and vegetables at all.

If you can afford organic produce for your family, then by all means, buy it. But many people simply cannot.

Here are some basic facts to put this in perspective…

The naturally occurring carcinogens in fruits and vegetables occur in parts of 1 per 10,000 or 1 per 100,000. That is a very high amount.

Plants naturally produce these toxic (and often carcinogenic) chemicals to kill micro-organisms and insects.

The synthetic carcinogens from pesticide residues occur in parts of 1 per 100 million or 1 per 1 billion, which is very small.

We are exposed to 10,000x more natural carcinogens in fruits and vegetables than synthetic ones.

The typical person consumes 750mg of naturally occurring carcinogens in a single day, verses 33mg of synthetic carcinogens in an entire YEAR.

On a day to day basis, you get about 0.09mg of carcinogens from synthetic pesticide residues, verses 750mg from natural carcinogens.

Most people are not aware of these numbers. You should at least be aware, regardless of your opinion on this topic.

References for all of this are below.

Response:

The text you provided is grounded in a well-known scientific argument primarily popularized by biochemist Bruce Ames and his colleagues in the early 1990s. While the figures are largely consistent with his widely cited research, there are important scientific nuances and updates to consider regarding "natural" versus "synthetic" hazards.

  1. Accuracy of the Ratios (10,000:1)

The claim that we are exposed to roughly 10,000 times more natural pesticides than synthetic ones is scientifically accurate according to the seminal paper "Dietary Pesticides (99.99% All Natural)" (Ames et al., 1990).

Scientific Grounding: Ames and Gold calculated that 99.99% (by weight) of the pesticides in the human diet are chemicals that plants produce naturally to defend themselves from fungi, insects, and predators (Ames et al., 1990).

Concentrations: The text mentions natural carcinogens occurring at 1 part per 10,000 and synthetic residues at 1 part per 100 million or 1 billion. This aligns with research stating that Americans consume approximately 1.5 grams of natural pesticides per day, compared to only about 0.09 mg (0.00009 g) of synthetic pesticide residues (Ames et al., 1990).

  1. Consumption Figures (750mg vs. 33mg)

The specific numbers in your text—750 mg per day of natural carcinogens versus 33 mg per year of synthetic ones—are the specific estimates used by Ames to highlight the "imbalance" in public perception of risk.

The 750mg figure: This represents the subset of the 1.5g of total natural pesticides that have been tested and found to be carcinogenic in high-dose rodent studies (Ames et al., 1990). For example, a single cup of coffee contains several natural chemicals (like caffeic acid) that are rodent carcinogens at high doses (Ames et al., 1990).

The 33mg figure: This is a calculated annual total for synthetic residues, which averages to less than 0.1 mg per day.

  1. Scientific Nuances and "Carcinogen" Labeling

While the math in your text is historically accurate to the source, modern toxicology applies several caveats to the term "carcinogen" as used here:

High-Dose Testing (The MTD): Many of these "natural carcinogens" were identified using the Maximum Tolerated Dose (MTD) in rodents—chronic, near-toxic doses that cause cell death and rapid cell division (mitogenesis) (Ames et al., 1990). Scientists argue that this doesn't necessarily mean these chemicals cause cancer at the low levels found in a normal human diet (Ames et al., 1990).

Protective Effects: Despite the presence of these natural chemicals, diets high in fruits and vegetables are consistently linked to a lower risk of cancer (Ames et al., 1990). This is likely because plants also contain vitamins, antioxidants, and fibers that counteract potential carcinogenic effects.

Equivalency: Research suggests that natural and synthetic chemicals are equally likely to test positive for carcinogenicity when subjected to high-dose animal tests (Ames et al., 1990). Therefore, the "synthetic" nature of a chemical is not, by itself, a predictor of its hazard level compared to "natural" ones.

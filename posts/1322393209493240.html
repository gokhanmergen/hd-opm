Some with high LDL avoid plaque, but heart disease is multifactorial and most aren't lucky

Louis Nervegna · Jun 19, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 6 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis recommends a short video (Dr. Daniel Chong with lipidologist Tom Dayspring) on why some people with high LDL avoid plaque. He stresses genetic protection is the exception; LDL/ApoB is one major factor among many, including insulin resistance, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and blood pressure.

  • Contributing factors: age, genetics, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative processes, high blood pressure, smoking, autoimmune and collagen diseases
  • Some people are genetically protected despite multiple risk factors

This is a very good video. It is short, and worth listening to.

The bottom line is that some lucky people are genetically-protected against heart disease, despite having multiple conventional risk factors.

They can get away with things that the rest of us cannot. Most of us are not this lucky.

Heart disease is multi-factorial: High LDL & ApoB are one major contributing factor. Other contributing factors are age, genetics & family history, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, endothelial disfunction, oxidative processes, high blood pressure, smoking, autoimmune diseases, collagen diseases, etc. It is a long list. And there are both contributing factors and protective factors that are not properly understood yet. Some haven’t even been identified yet.

Shared from Vital Human- Dr. Daniel Chong · original post

WHY DO SOME PEOPLE WITH HIGH LDL NOT GET PLAQUE?

I recall a time many years ago when I would tell patients things like "all lipidologists care about is LDL" and "all lipidologists want to do is give you statins". Then, I started actually training with them and listening directly to them at length, and what I discovered as actually the opposite. In lipidology and preventive cardiology we fully acknowledge the role LDL plays, but also acknowledge (and work on) all the other angles of contribution to plaque and heart attacks/strokes that can be at play in any one individual person.

One lipidologist I have learned more from than any other is Tom Dayspring. Listen to him here to see what I'm talking about.

Stuart Phillips: little evidence 1.6 g/kg beats 1.2 g/kg protein; 1 g/lb 'ridiculous'

Louis Nervegna · Mar 30, 2026 · ♥ 4 reactions · 2 comments · original Facebook post

Louis argues influencers misquote exercise scientist Stuart Phillips. Quoted, Phillips says that for most people the difference between 1.2 and 1.6 g/kg is about 15-25 g of protein per day and that consuming 2.2 g/kg (1 g/lb) is ridiculous with no supporting evidence.

Influencers like Peter Attia who push high protein for “longevity” misrepresent and misquote the work of exercise scientist Stuart Phillips.

What does Stuart Philips actually say when asked about this?

He says that there is NOT strong evidence that increasing protein intake from 1.2g/kg to 1.6g/kg will make much difference for most people. And he says that it is “ridiculous” to consume 1g/lb of protein. He emphasizes that there is “no evidence” to support consuming 1g/lb of protein.

(For reference, 1.2g/kg is a little less than the average American eats every day, anyway: ↪ The US leads the world in protein intake, about 1.5 g/kg per day.)

You can see Stuart Phillip’s protein recommendations in the following three posts:

PART 1: ↪ Phillips: 74 trials show no extra muscle gain above 1.6 g/kg/day protein with lifting

PART 2: ↪ Stuart Phillips: the 1.6 g/kg protein ceiling holds for older adults; women may need less

PART 3: ↪ Protein above 1.6 g/kg adds no benefit even in older adults who lift, says Stuart Phillips

Quoted:

Simon Hill: “Is there any evidence that consuming 1.6g/kg is significantly better than 1.2g/kg? So not comparing 1.6 to 0.8 which are the guidelines, but comparing it to 1.2 which is closer to where people are actually landing naturally in western populations?”

Stuart Phillips: “Simon, strongly no. But evidence there is… is attached. The degree of debate over 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein, however, amounts to an additional 15-25g of protein per day depending on body weight. Scorched earth nuance, in my opinion. What is very clear, at least to me, is that it is ridiculous to be consuming 2.2g/kg or 1g/lb or more. There is no evidence to support this, even in energy restricted states and even for muscle.”

After reanalysis, Keto-CTA plaque growth still matches untreated metabolic syndrome

Louis Nervegna · Mar 02, 2026 · ♥ 9 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis argues the reanalyzed Keto-CTA plaque growth in the lean mass hyper-responder cohort is still as bad as that of people with untreated metabolic syndrome and prediabetes on a standard diet in the PARADIGM study, moving from 'horrible' to 'just plain bad'. PARADIGM used the same QAngio software as the reanalysis.

  • PARADIGM: thousands of patients, 13 sites, 7 countries, serial CT imaging
  • Same QAngio software used in KETO-CTA reanalysis

Even after re-analysis with QAngio software, the plaque growth in the Keto-CTA (LMHR) study is still bad [orange].

The plaque growth is as bad as people with untreated metabolic syndrome and prediabetes eating the standard American diet [gold], in the PARADIGM study.

After re-analysis, the results went from horrible [red] to just plain bad [orange].

Quoted:

“The PARADIGM study is one of the most rigorous serial coronary imaging studies ever conducted. It tracked plaque progression in thousands of patients across 13 sites in 7 countries, using the same quantitative software — QAngio, made by Medis Medical Imaging — that was later used to reanalyze the KETO-CTA data.

In PARADIGM, metabolically healthy individuals with no risk factors [eating SAD diet] progressed at approximately 3.2 cubic millimeters of soft plaque per year [green]. Individuals with metabolic syndrome — pre-diabetic, often hypertensive, with poor lipid profiles — progressed at 5.6 cubic millimeters per year [gold].

The LMHR cohort progressed at 18.8 cubic millimeters per year [red]. In people selected specifically for optimal metabolic health. More than three times the metabolic syndrome rate. Even the reanalysis, which has not been peer reviewed, showed progression at 5.5 cubic millimeters per year in the LMHR group [orange].”

My note:

Compare the progressions in the quote above to people in the PARADIGM trial who were properly treated with statins [blue]. Their progression was only 0.5 cubic millimeters per year. They progressed at less than 1/10th the rate of people on a keto diet in the Keto-CTA study, even after re-analysis with QAngio software [orange].

For further comparison, a DASH diet plus statins produced a -39.5 cubic millimeter REGRESSION of soft plaque in the DISCO-CT trial. See these past posts:

Repatha alone reversed soft plaque in 37 of 47 patients over 18 months at LDL near 30

Nov 13, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 1 PCSK9, ezetimibe & newer drugs

Louis explains a small uncontrolled study of the PCSK9 inhibitor evolocumab with CT angiography. Average LDL was about 30 mg/dL, 37 of 47 patients had some non-calcified plaque regression, and many had large reversals worth years of typical growth (about 5 mm3 per year). He cautions it was small with no randomization or control group, and that PCSK9 monotherapy is not yet proven to reduce heart attacks.

Keto-CTA reanalysis: plaque progression still no better than the standard American diet

Louis compares reanalyses of the Keto-CTA (lean mass hyper-responder) study by different CT software with the Nature-CT standard American diet group. Keto-CTA plaque growth matched diabetics' and far exceeded high-risk statin-treated groups

Apr 16, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 4 · 💬 1 Plaque, CAC & CT angiography

Louis quotes an analysis of the Keto-CTA trial putting its plaque progression in context: median non-calcified plaque rose 18.8 mm3 in one year, comparable to type 2 diabetics (~17.4 mm3/yr), about triple PARADIGM metabolically normal people, and nearly 3x SMARTool high-risk non-statin users (6.4 mm3/yr). PAV rose 0.8% overall and 2.4% in those with CAC over 100.

Keto-CTA reanalysis with new software: plaque progression matches the standard diet group

Sep 05, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 4 · 💬 3 Low-carb, keto & carnivore

Louis explains that Keto-CTA originally showed 3.8x worse plaque progression than a matched SAD group, and a reanalysis with different software now shows roughly equal progression. He argues this is still a bad result and that both studies must be reanalyzed with the same software for a fair comparison.

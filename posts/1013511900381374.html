'Cough CPR' for heart attacks is a myth; chew aspirin and call 911

Louis Nervegna · Apr 24, 2025 · ♥ 16 reactions · 10 comments · original Facebook post
Heart attack, stroke & proceduresMyths, misinformation & controversiesAspirin, antiplatelets & blood thinners
AI summary & key facts

Sharing an American Heart Association piece debunking the idea that vigorous coughing can treat a heart attack. Louis adds the correct response: chew a full-strength aspirin, call 911 and unlock the door in case you pass out.

  • Cough CPR does not work for self-treating a heart attack
  • Chew full-strength aspirin and call 911

Chew and swallow a full strength aspirin, and call 911.

Make sure your doors are unlocked, in case you pass out while waiting for help to arrive.

Can someone having a heart attack treat themselves with vigorous coughing? In a word, no.

Misinformation has circulated for years on social media about “cough CPR.” Doctors debunk the myth and share what you should really do.

NYU researchers developed PRESS, combining platelet aggregation responses and RNA sequencing, to detect platelet hyperreactivity. In PAD patients, hyperreactive platelets predicted more major adverse cardiac or limb events within 30 days of revascularization, and PRESS above the median carried a 90% higher risk of major events over 18 months. Louis shares a report on a meta-analysis of 155 studies finding that common viral infections raise heart attack and stroke risk, both soon after infection and long term. Flu carried up to six times higher heart attack risk the month after infection, and COVID-19 nearly doubled risk.

Heart attack clots form on ruptured unstable plaque, not in plaque-free arteries

Mar 17, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 29 · 💬 12 Heart attack, stroke & procedures

Louis argues that although most heart attacks are caused by clots, clots form when unstable plaque ruptures, so people with near-zero plaque have near-zero risk of clot-caused heart attacks. Blood thinners like aspirin are proven to lower risk in people with considerable plaque but are unlikely to help, and may harm, those with little or no plaque.

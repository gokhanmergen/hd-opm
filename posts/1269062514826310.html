“Lac‑Phe is produced when the body is under metabolic demand. It forms when lactate—a substance that accumulates during exertion—combines with phenylalanine, a basic building block of protein. Scientists first took notice of Lac‑Phe because its levels spike after intense exercise, coinciding with shifts in energy use and appetite regulation.

In preclinical and early human studies, higher Lac‑Phe levels have been associated with reduced appetite and improved weight control—two effects commonly linked to regular physical activity.

Lac-Phe does not rise only with exercise. Scientists observed elevated Lac-Phe levels in people taking metformin, even in the absence of physical activity.”

A diabetes drug millions already take may replicate what happens inside your body during exercise.

Scientists at Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center, part of the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine, published a study in April 2026 in the journal EMBO Molecular Medicine that stopped the research world in its tracks.

They found that metformin, one of the most prescribed medications on the planet with over 150 million prescriptions written every year, raises levels of a molecule called Lac-Phe in patients who are completely inactive.

Lac-Phe, known in full as N-lactoyl-phenylalanine, is a compound that forms when lactate, the byproduct of physical exertion, combines with phenylalanine, an amino acid. Scientists have long known that Lac-Phe spikes dramatically after intense exercise and is directly tied to appetite regulation and weight control.

But then the team discovered something remarkable. Prostate cancer patients taking metformin who were not exercising at all showed Lac-Phe levels that matched those previously recorded after strenuous physical activity.

The reason connects to how metformin works at a cellular level. The drug inhibits complex 1 of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which forces the body into a simulated low-energy state. This activates AMPK, the same energy-sensing enzyme that exercise triggers. In other words, metformin fools the body into thinking it has just worked out.

The finding matters most for people whose conditions prevent them from exercising. Hormone therapy for prostate cancer causes severe fatigue that stops patients from staying active, cutting off one of the most reliable tools for managing weight and metabolic health during treatment.

But then there is a complication the headlines are not always mentioning. Separate research from Rutgers University, led by professor Steven Malin and published in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism, found that metformin can actually blunt the benefits of real exercise. Patients on the drug who exercised did not gain the same cardiovascular and blood vessel improvements as those who exercised without it.

So the picture is more complicated than a simple exercise pill. Metformin may offer metabolic benefits when you cannot move, but it may limit the gains you make when you can.

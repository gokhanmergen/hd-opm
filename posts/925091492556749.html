Gokhan questions the belief that sugar and carbs cause diabetes, citing a person who resolved diabetes on a high-carb, very low-fat diet and Denise Minger's article on low-fat diets with diabetes remission. His argument rests on an anecdote and a blog, not controlled trial data.

  • Anecdote: diabetes resolved on a high-carb, no-fat diet
  • Cites studies of diabetes remission with high sugar intake when fat is avoided and calories controlled

Does Dietary Sugar Cause Diabetes?

We have folks like Bg Sharma who eat a high carb diet, and resolved his diabetes 100% (as long as he keeps avoiding all fat). His labs, in general, are some of the best I've ever seen without any drugs or supplements (ok he's still taking metformin, I think).

I believe this should be some food for thought for those of us who believe in blood sugar spikes and, in effect, most carbs are causing diabetes.

Someone jokingly called his diet "carbosis" 🙂 (Similar to ketosis but the opposite). There are actual studies on it, where the participants are eating plenty of sugar, yet they're experiencing diabetes remission (as long as they avoid all the fat and control calories).

So, if you're under the impression that sugar, and in effect carbs are the evil, and "ketosis" (complete carb avoidance) is the only way to resolve your metabolic disturbances, such studies can be a data point.

The Lost Science of Low-Fat: Rediscovering Forgotten Evidence in Nutritional Medicine

This comprehensive article challenges common beliefs about low-fat diets and presents historical evidence of their therapeutic effectiveness. The author examines several pioneering doctors from the mid-20th century who successfully used very low-fat diets (around 10% of calories from fat) to treat various diseases. These include Walter Kempner's rice diet, which reversed diabetes and hypertension; Roy Swank's work showing dramatic improvements in multiple sclerosis patients; and Nathan Pritikin's program that helped reverse heart disease and other chronic conditions. The article emphasizes that these results occurred well before Ancel Keys' more famous low-fat advocacy.

The author makes a crucial distinction between truly low-fat diets (around 10% of calories from fat) and what is commonly considered "low-fat" today (30% of calories from fat). She argues that most modern studies dismissing low-fat diets are actually testing diets in what she calls the "Macronutrient Swampland" - neither low enough in fat to achieve therapeutic effects nor high enough to enter ketosis. The article presents extensive clinical evidence showing that very low-fat diets could improve or reverse conditions like diabetes, heart disease, and multiple sclerosis, even when the diets included refined carbohydrates and sugar.

The piece concludes by acknowledging that the author previously dismissed low-fat diets too quickly and apologizes for not examining the historical evidence more thoroughly before drawing conclusions. She emphasizes that while this doesn't mean low-fat diets are the only effective therapeutic approach, the evidence for their benefits cannot be ignored or rationalized away. The article challenges both the low-carb and vegan communities by showing that neither carbohydrates (even refined ones) nor animal products appear to be the decisive factors in the documented health improvements, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of nutrition is needed.

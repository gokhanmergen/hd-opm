Declining proteolysis in aging: muscle loss and protein aggregation

Gokhan Can · Jan 11, 2024 · ♥ 16 reactions · 18 comments · original Facebook post
Aging & longevityBrain health & dementia
AI summary & key facts

Explains how falling protein-breakdown activity contributes to age-related problems such as sarcopenia and the accumulation of misfolded proteins in Alzheimer's disease, backed by several review papers. It is general aging biology with little direct heart-disease content.

  • Reduced proteolysis lets damaged proteins accumulate in muscle, contributing to sarcopenia
  • Misfolded protein accumulation is a hallmark of Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases

Proteolysis

The decline in proteolytic activity, the process by which enzymes break down proteins, contributes to numerous age-related issues:

1. Loss of Muscle Mass (Sarcopenia): Proteolysis is essential for removing damaged proteins in muscle cells. With reduced proteolytic activity, these proteins accumulate, leading to muscle loss and weakness.

2. Protein Aggregation in Neurodegenerative Diseases: Diseases like Alzheimer's are characterized by the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the brain. Proteolytic enzymes typically degrade these misfolded proteins. A decrease in proteolytic activity can lead to their accumulation, forming plaques common in various neurodegenerative diseases.

3. Impaired Cellular Function: Proteolytic activity is essential for maintaining cellular balance by degrading damaged or obsolete proteins. A reduction in this activity with age can result in an accumulation of damaged proteins within cells, impairing cellular function and contributing to aging and age-related diseases.

Paper · Nat Commun · 2014The role of protein clearance mechanisms in organismal ageing and age-related diseasesnature.com Paper · Nat Rev Mol Cell Biol · 2019The proteostasis network and its decline in ageingnature.com

4. Atherosclerosis (Plaque in Arteries): Proteolytic enzymes are involved in the progression and stability of atherosclerotic plaques. They are essential for the degradation of the extracellular matrix in arterial walls. Impaired or unbalanced proteolytic activity can contribute to plaque development. Additionally, a decrease in certain proteolytic activities can cause arterial wall thickening and reduced elasticity, worsening atherosclerosis.

Meta-analysis of 69 RCTs: protein strength gains plateau at 1.5 g/kg and need lifting

Jul 26, 2024 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 10 · 💬 6 ↩ 11 Protein

A dose-response meta-analysis of 69 randomized trials found protein alone does not increase muscle strength without resistance training. With training, strength rose 0.72% per extra 0.1 g/kg/day up to 1.5 g/kg/day, with no further gain; going from the RDA to 1.5 g/kg adds only about 5% more strength.

No drug is approved for sarcopenia; strength training and protein remain the foundation

A review summary (Rolland et al., Metabolism, Dec 2023) of drug options for age-related muscle loss. No drug has regulatory approval for sarcopenia, few trials enrolled diagnosed patients, and non-drug measures remain first-line.

Athletes in their 70s and 80s have leg muscle density similar to athletes in their 40s

May 16, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 13 · 💬 5 Exercise & fitness

Louis shares University of Pittsburgh MRI findings in 40 high-level recreational athletes aged 40-81 training 4-5 times per week. Muscle density and strength held steady with consistent activity, suggesting much age-related decline is due to inactivity. Sedentary adults may lose up to 8% of muscle per decade after 40.

Statin muscle pain is common in some, severe damage is rare; statins may aid sarcopenia

Jan 02, 2025 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 7 · 💬 15 Statins

Louis argues statins are not toxic to muscle in most people. Myalgia occurs in some, possibly with a genetic susceptibility, while rhabdomyolysis is very rare (about 1 in 100,000, or 1 in 10,000 conservatively). Switching statins (e.g., to pravastatin or pitavastatin) or using non-statin LDL drugs can help, and low-dose statins are being studied as a treatment for sarcopenia.

More protein without resistance training did not improve muscle in older adults

Louis Nervegna · Apr 05, 2026 · ♥ 5 reactions · 6 comments · original Facebook post
ProteinExercise & fitness
AI summary & key facts

In a 24-week factorial RCT of healthy older adults, increasing protein from about 0.85 to 1.5 g/kg/day with whey, with or without potassium bicarbonate, did not improve muscle power, physical performance or mass, though IGF-1 rose. Louis adds it to evidence that extra protein alone does not build muscle.

  • 24 weeks, 0.85 to 1.5 g/kg/day protein
  • No gain in muscle power, performance or mass
  • Circulating IGF-1 increased

Add this to the massive pile of studies that shows increasing protein consumption above the RDA does absolutely nothing to improve muscle strength, muscle mass, power, or performance— in the absence of resistance training.

A review of those studies can be found here: ↪ Meta-analysis of 69 RCTs: protein strength gains plateau at 1.5 g/kg and need lifting.

This study again shows it is true, even in OLDER adults, as multiple other studies have also demonstrated: facebook.com/share/v/1B47Wxg9yx.

But what increasing protein consumption from 0.85g/kg (~RDA) to 1.5g/kg clearly did do in this study is raise circulating levels of IGF1.

In this case, the study used whey protein (WP), which is particularly potent at raising IGF1, due its strong insulinogenic effect: ↪ Willett shares concern that whey powders may raise long-term risk via insulin/IGF1/mTOR.

High levels of IGF1 have been repeatedly linked to increased cancer risk in humans in large prospective cohort studies. Humans with genetically low IGF1 almost never get cancer (Laron Syndrone): ↪ Laron syndrome, with lifelong low IGF-1 and mTOR, shows low cancer, diabetes and CVD risk. And genetic interventions to lower IGF1 consistently extend lifespan in mice and other animal models. The longest lived lab mouse on record (5 years) was an IGF1 knockout.

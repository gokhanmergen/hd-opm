It’s untrue that paleolithic humans ate mostly ketogenic or carnivore (as opposed to omnivore) diets.

In addition to eating meat and fish when opportunistically available, these early humans likely consumed large amounts of plant foods, including starches :

Scientific AmericanScientists Find Early Evidence of Humans Cooking StarchesMore than 100 millennia ago, people were roasting tubers—a practice that fueled their bodies and may have aided migrationsscientificamerican.com Paper · The Quarterly Review of Biology · 2015The Importance of Dietary Carbohydrate in Human Evolutionjournals.uchicago.edu

The idea that our paleolithic ancestors did not consume significant amounts of starch is likely wrong, according to Harvard anthropologist and primatologist Richard Wrangham.

He summarizes the evidence for this in his book “Catching Fire: How Cooking Made us Human”.

en.wikipedia.orgCatching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human - Wikipediaen.wikipedia.org

Wrangham explains in the book that cooking and eating starchy tubers and roots is likely a major reason why human brains evolved into a larger size. The human brain operates primarily on glucose, which starch readily provides. Cooking the tubers and roots before eating them releases the starch and makes it much more bioavailable during digestion. According to Wrangham, starchy tubers and roots were staples in the hunter-gatherer human diet. He explains that Paleolithic humans were not carnivores, but rather omnivores who cooked much of their animal/plant food to make the calories more bioavailable. These early humans hunted and cooked meat and fish opportunistically and seasonally when it was available. Starchy tubers and roots, however, were almost always available underground, even when meat and fish was not.

I will quote from his Wikipedia page:

“Wrangham focused recently on the role cooking has played in human evolution. In Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human, he argued that cooking food is obligatory for humans as a result of biological adaptations and that cooking, in particular the consumption of cooked tubers, might explain the increase in hominid brain sizes, smaller teeth and jaws, and decrease in sexual dimorphism that occurred roughly 1.8 million years ago.”

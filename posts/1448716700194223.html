Extra protein cannot be stored: the body burns it for energy or converts it to fat

Louis Nervegna · Nov 12, 2026 · ♥ 1 reactions · 0 comments · original Facebook post
Protein
AI summary & key facts

Louis shares a clip of Stanford's Christopher Gardner explaining that unlike fat, the body has no dedicated storage for excess dietary protein. Surplus amino acids are broken down and used for energy, with carbon skeletons possibly becoming glucose or fat. Adequate protein matters, but more is not automatically better.

  • No dedicated storage system for excess dietary protein
  • Excess amino acids can be burned for energy or contribute to glucose or fat production

You can’t store extra protein.

Any excess gets burned as energy, or stored as fat.

Stanford professor Christopher Gardner explains it in this video clip.

Quoted from the original post:

Think eating extra protein today means your body can save it for tomorrow? Think again.

Unlike fat, your body doesn’t have a dedicated storage system for excess dietary protein.

Your body is constantly using amino acids to build and repair tissues, produce enzymes, and support essential functions. But when you consume more protein than your body needs, those extra amino acids don’t simply sit in a reserve waiting for your next workout.

Instead, they can be broken down and used for energy, with their carbon skeletons potentially contributing to glucose or fat production.

So while getting enough protein matters, more isn’t automatically better.

Stuart Phillips: 1.6 g/kg/day protein remains sufficient for people who resistance train

Louis shares a response from protein researcher Stuart Phillips defending the 1.6 g/kg/day recommendation after a critique. He argues it is a sufficiency with a margin, not a cliff, and that head-to-head trials of 1.6 versus up to 3.2 g/kg/day found no significant lean-mass advantage, while three studies cited for more benefit used weak muscle measures.

Phillips posted a new, not peer-reviewed preprint reviewing the evidence and concluding that the muscle-related benefit of protein still tops out at about 1.6 g/kg per day, and only in people doing resistance training.

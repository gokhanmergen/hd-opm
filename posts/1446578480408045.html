Atorvastatin may 'disarm' Lp(a) by blocking oxidation, a theory for HORIZON's failure

Louis Nervegna · Nov 10, 2026 · ♥ 9 reactions · 12 comments · original Facebook post
Lp(a)Statins
AI summary & key facts

Louis proposes that Lp(a)HORIZON failed because aggressive statin treatment (78% on high-intensity statins) may have already neutralized oxidized phospholipids carried by Lp(a). A shared abstract (Sherratt et al., Eur Heart J) found atorvastatin's active metabolite scavenges free radicals and inhibits oxidation of Lp(a) and small dense LDL better than other statins. This is a lab-based hypothesis.

  • 78% of Lp(a)HORIZON patients were on high-intensity statins
  • Atorvastatin metabolite inhibited oxidation of Lp(a) and small dense LDL in the lab
  • Outperformed other statins in this antioxidant effect
  • Statins often slightly raise Lp(a) particle numbers

One of the theories why the Lp(a) HORIZON trial failed is because the aggressive treatment of patients with statins and other drugs might have neutralized the deleterious effects of OxPL carried by Lp(a) particles, rendering the lowering of Lp(a) unnecessary.

Lp(a)'s harmful effects may actually stem from the oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) that it carries: facebook.com/share/p/1JEgpfYVSK.

Neutralizing these oxidized phospholipids might cancel the harm of high Lp(a).

Sam Tsimikas has emphasized this in the past, and has recently brought it up again on Twitter: ↪ Tsimikas: Lp(a) risk can be managed with LDL under 65 and tight risk-factor control.

78% of the patients in HORIZON were on high-intensity statins, and most of the rest were at least taking lower doses.

Atorvastatin seems to be particularly effective at neutralizing OxPL.

Colloquially speaking, atorvastatin might "disarm" the Lp(a) particles.

Atorvastatin might make the Lp(a) particles less dangerous by preventing them from carrying the oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) that cause damage to arteries and promote clotting.

This specific study (Sherratt et al., published as an abstract in the European Heart Journal) provides a very compelling mechanistic argument for using atorvastatin over other statins if your primary concern is Lp(a) and OxPL-ApoB.

The trial actually shows
The study looked at how different statins interact with the "oxidation" process of cholesterol particles.

  • The Findings: Atorvastatin (specifically its active hydroxylated metabolite) acts as a free radical scavenger. In the lab, it significantly inhibited the oxidation of Lp(a) and small-dense LDL.
  • The Comparison: It outperformed other statins (like pitavistatin or rosuvastatin) in this specific antioxidant capacity.
  • The Conclusion: The researchers argue that atorvastatin might "disarm" the Lp(a) particle. Even if the statin doesn't lower the number of Lp(a) particles (and statins often slightly increase the number), atorvastatin may make the particles you have less toxic by preventing them from carrying the oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) that cause arterial damage.

Lp(a) makes clots form more easily and resist breakdown via OxPL and apo(a)

Nov 10, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 3 Lp(a)

Louis shares a summary of an ATVB commentary by Rafael Zubiran explaining why high Lp(a) may be worse than LDL. Its oxidized phospholipids activate monocytes to make tissue factor, and apo(a) resembles plasminogen and competes with clot breakdown, producing denser, harder-to-dissolve clots. Louis notes statins, especially atorvastatin, may neutralize OxPL and aspirin lowers clotting risk.

Big swings in Lp(a) usually reflect acute inflammation, not an intervention working

Jun 20, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 11 · 💬 4 ↩ 1 Lp(a)

Louis explains that Lp(a) is largely genetic but behaves as an acute-phase reactant, so infection, surgery, extreme exercise, vaccine reactions or inflammatory flares can shift test results temporarily. Large changes are therefore usually due to inflammation, and blaming big jumps on statins can hide an underlying problem.

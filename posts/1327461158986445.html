Niacin (nicotinic acid) showed promise in a small Parkinson's trial, likely via brain NAD

Louis highlights a small 2021 human trial of niacin in Parkinson's disease, noting it is the form of vitamin B3 once used to lower cholesterol and that benefit may work through raising NAD in the brain. Nicotinamide riboside has similar small-trial results, including a 2023 high-dose safety trial.

  • Small human trial published 2021
  • Mechanism appears to involve raising NAD levels
  • Nicotinamide riboside also shows promise in small human trials (NR-SAFE, Nature Communications 2023)

Quoted:

32 men and 15 women with an average age of 62 years and a diagnosis of Parkinson's disease were enrolled in this double-blind study [1]. Along with their regular medications, they took daily supplements of either a placebo, 100 mg of niacin, or 250 mg of slow-release niacin. Each patient was assessed before treatment and after three months. Clinical assessments were carried out including disease-specific measures, such as the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale III (UPDRS) and more general quality of life measures, such as the Fatigue Severity Scale. Blood was also collected from the patients and analyzed for niacin plasma levels and inflammatory cytokines.

Following the results of the 3 month study, all subjects began taking 250 mg of slow-release niacin daily. If they were already on that regimen, they remained so for 9 more months for a total of one year. Those in the placebo and 100 mg groups took the 250 mg supplement for 12 more months. All measures were taken again after 12 months of 250 mg slow-release niacin.

The study's primary endpoint, the UPDRS, showed a high amount of variability at 3 months, but it improved from baseline at 12 months. Although there was no control group at the 12-month time point, scores improved by 3.5 points, while a decline of 5.5 points would have been expected over the same time period in a similar study population.

Many secondary measures also improved, including handwriting, perception of fatigue, mood, postural control, and frontal EEG rhythm. Several other measures, such as quality of sleep, did not change after 12 months of niacin treatment. These may or may not represent benefits, since declines might reasonably be expected based on disease progression. Only one measure was poorer compared to baseline, an assessment of visual attention and task switching known as the Trail Making Test.

“We have demonstrated the potential effectiveness of over-the-counter niacin enhancement as a proof of concept to support the well-being of individuals with PD. Vitamin B3 augmentation has the potential to maintain or improve symptoms. Based on the results of this effectiveness exploratory trial, a larger multicenter RCT is warranted.”

Blood NAD levels do not fall with age, so commercial NAD blood tests are useless

Oct 24, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 15 · 💬 5 Aging & longevity

A new Nature Metabolism study found whole-blood NAD+ levels stay stable with age across seven independent human cohorts. Louis explains blood NAD mainly reflects recent vitamin B3 intake, so commercial NAD blood tests are not meaningful, while tissue NAD levels may matter but cannot be commercially measured. There is still no convincing clinical evidence NR or NMN supplements slow human aging.

Whole-blood NAD+ does not change with age or lifestyle, so it is a poor aging marker

May 22, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 5 · 💬 4 Aging & longevity

Louis, echoing Charles Brenner, shares a Nature Metabolism paper reporting that human whole-blood NAD+ is stable across age and not altered by exercise or lifestyle interventions. Nicotinamide riboside raised NAD+, but that does not prove clinical or longevity benefit; tissue NAD matters.

Blood NAD+ is a useless aging biomarker: stable across seven cohorts (Nature Metabolism)

May 14, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 5 Aging & longevity

Louis argues that Charles Brenner is vindicated by a Nature Metabolism study quantifying NAD+ in seven human cohorts by mass spectrometry. Whole-blood NAD+ stayed stable with age and lifestyle and changed only with nicotinamide riboside supplementation, so blood NAD+ tests do not reflect tissue levels.

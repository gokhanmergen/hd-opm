Mouse taurine doses of 500-1000 mg/kg scale to about 2.8-5.7 g for a 70 kg human

Louis Nervegna · Oct 26, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 7 comments · original Facebook post
Aging & longevitySupplements & vitamins
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that the 2023 Columbia taurine study used 500-1000 mg/kg in mice, and that allometric scaling for faster mouse metabolism converts this to about 2.8-5.7 g for a 70 kg person. He suggests that is the range to test in human trials, since mouse mg/kg doses cannot be applied directly to humans.

  • Mouse dose 500-1000 mg/kg
  • Allometric conversion: about 2.8-5.7 g for a 70 kg human
  • Doses cannot simply be copied per kg from mice to humans

This 2023 Columbia University study used a taurine dose of 500-1000mg/kg in mice.

After appropriate allometric scaling, this converts to a dose of about 2.8-5.7g for a 70kg human.

This 2.8-5.7g dose range would be appropriate doses to test in human clinical trials to try to replicate the findings in mice.

Because mice have much faster metabolism than humans, you cannot simply use the same dose/kg in humans. You must apply an allometric scaling factor to adjust for the difference in metabolism. The FDA provides guidelines on how to do that for clinical trials (see comments).

Here is the math according to the FDA guidelines for allometrically scaling mouse doses to humans:

Divide 500-1000mg/kg by 12.3 to allometrically scale. This gives you a dose range of 40.7-81.3mg/kg for humans. Multiply that by 70kg to get 2.8-5.7g.

This 2.8-5.7g dose range is in line with taurine doses used in previous human studies, although on the high end. And it is in the range of what people actually take (e.g. athletes) when they supplement with taurine, although again on the high end.

Please note that this allometric scaling only gives you a very crude estimate of what might work in humans. It is only a starting point that needs to be refined by appropriately testing dose ranges in human clinical trials. This analysis is only meant to give you a rough feeling of what doses might work in humans. Emphasis on might.

  • Taurine deficiency as a driver of aging (Columbia University) · other · 2023
Shared from Louis Nervegna · original post

This study at Columbia University on the amino acid taurine and its effects on the aging process in worms, mice, monkeys, and humans, attracted a large amount of attention this past summer (2023).

Yet many people here are probably not aware of it.

Given that taurine also has beneficial effects on the heart and cardiovascular system, this study is very relevant to the group.

Age is by far the biggest causal risk factor for heart disease. Interventions that target the aging process of the cardiovascular system itself are the future of cardiology. The research is still in its infancy, but taurine might be one of those interventions. Emphasis on might. Most importantly, it appears to be reasonably safe. (It is naturally produced by the human body and found in food.)

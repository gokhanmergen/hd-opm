Why does the LMHR phenotype exist?

Why does it occur in very lean people on ketogenic/carnivore diets?

Why is it more prevalent in ApoE4 genotypes?

Why does it lead to insanely high LDL-C. Sometimes as high as 500 mg/dL or even 1000 mg/dL?

Why is there a higher probability of it occurring if the strict keto diet is combined with fasting and intense exercise in people who are already very lean?

——

An explanation comes from the existing medical literature on extreme calorie restriction:

When you have near zero body fat and you completely eliminate all carbohydrate and force yourself always into ketosis, your body can be tricked into believing that it is starving. When your body thinks that it is starving, it dramatically increases the amount of circulating LDL particles as a way to deliver more fat as energy to potentially starving tissues.

In this case, your body also decreases thyroid function in an attempt to slow metabolism and hold onto every calorie possible. It also lowers sex hormones like testosterone in an attempt to delay sexual reproduction until food is more available (which increases the chances of offspring surviving).

It’s an ancient defense mechanism against starvation that evolved in mammals millions of years ago— and it was well known in the medical literature long before “LMHR”. It has been largely forgotten because starvation is very rare in modern times. Now it is being rediscovered again with all this “LMHR” stuff and the popularity of ketogenic diets which can mimic aspects of starvation (like extremely high circulating LDL) in very lean people.

PaperScienceDirect articlesciencedirect.com

Note that these are not necessarily bad things. In fact, calorie restriction has been shown to extend lifespan in nearly every animal species ever tested. It’s possible that certain kinds of ketogenic diets might also extend lifespan by mimicking some aspects of calorie restriction, as explained above. The potential danger comes from overdoing it. Calorie restriction extends lifespan in animals, but full blown starvation obviously shortens it. So it’s important not to overdo it.

ApoE4 evolved as a mechanism to help protect against starvation via increasing circulation of LDL and other particles capable of delivering fat as energy. That’s why it was selected for over the course of millions of years of evolution. And that’s why this LMHR phenomenon is more common in people with those genes who are lean and go on strict ketogenic diets.

LDL helps transport fatty acids and cholesterol around the body. During starvation, LDL works to shuttle fats released from adipose tissue to organs to provide fuel. The increased demand raises LDL production. On a strict keto diet, the extra LDL can be needed to shuttle excess fat from the diet for energy because no carbohydrate is available just like in starvation.

Starvation decreases leptin levels. Leptin normally upregulates LDL receptors. With lower receptors, LDL clearance from blood decreases.

Ketone production and ketosis during starvation places increased demand on the liver to synthesize cholesterol. This raises LDL as cholesterol is transported from the liver. The same happens on a keto diet.

Weight loss from extreme dieting causes LDL levels to rise due to lipids mobilized from fat cells entering plasma temporarily before clearance. The same happens on a keto diet.

Starvation decreases production of lipoprotein lipase, making it harder to clear LDL from circulation.

If you are very lean and combine a strict keto diet with fasting and high intensity exercise or other interventions that mimic calorie restriction (e.g. the drug rapamycin), you increase the odds that your body is tricked into believing that it is starving. This further increases the chances of LDL going up as a defense mechanism.

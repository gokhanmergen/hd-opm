White potatoes: short-term endothelial decline (MODERATE) and more hypertension in cohorts

White potatoes and endothelial function
The MODERATE randomized trial (Modifiable Effectors of Renin System Activation Treatment Evaluation) was a clinical study designed to investigate, in part, how diet affects endothelial-dependent vasodilation, a key marker of vascular health.

The trial specifically used brachial artery ultrasonography to measure how blood vessels dilate, which is a physiological process regulated by the renin-angiotensin system. The specific technique used to measure short term dietary changes was flow mediated dilation (FMD).

Data from the MODERATE trial indicated that participants who consumed one or more servings of boiled, baked, or mashed potatoes in the previous 24 hours had a 1.7% lower endothelial-dependent vasodilation compared to non-consumers.

To put this result in perspective, endothelial function generally declines by about 1.2% for every 10 years of age. If the 1.7% decline were to persist in the long term, it would be equivalent to about 14 years of natural aging. Essentially, a single day of potato consumption temporarily mirrored the endothelial function of someone 14 years older.

The important caveat here is that this was a SHORT TERM change. It’s unclear whether frequent white potato consumption would actually produce this same decline in the longer term. This is an important question for future studies.

Hypertension is largely driven by long term declines in endothelial function. The short-term vascular changes observed in the MODERATE trial align with findings from three massive long-term studies: the Harvard Nurses' Health Study (I and II) and the Harvard Health Professionals Follow-up Study:

PaperThe BMJ articlebmj.com

Consuming 4 or more servings per week of baked, boiled, or mashed potatoes in these cohort studies was associated with an 11% higher risk of developing hypertension.

Replacing one serving of potatoes per day with a non-starchy vegetable was associated with a 7% lower risk of developing high blood pressure.

Experts from Harvard's T.H. Chan School of Public Health suggest that the white starch in potatoes may be the primary driver:

The Nutrition Source - Harvard Chan SchoolAre Potatoes Healthy? • The Nutrition SourcePotatoes are a global favorite food, but not all preparations are an optimal part of a healthy eating pattern. A look at the research on potatoes and health.nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu

Metabolically, the white starch in potatoes behaves similar to the white starch found in refined grains like white bread, white rice, white pasta, etc. Blood sugar and blood insulin spikes from refined starch can lead to oxidative stress (ROS), and inflammation, which impairs the small arteries' ability to dilate properly. ROS molecules neutralize Nitric Oxide. Since Nitric Oxide is the primary signaling molecule that tells arteries to dilate, its depletion leads to impaired vasodilation.

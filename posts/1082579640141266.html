The idea that reliable medical knowledge is achieved only through randomized controlled trials is wrong.

Two famous examples:

  1. Smoking—

There has never been a randomized controlled trial in humans that showed smoking causes lung cancer.

  1. Trans Fats—

There has never been a randomized controlled trial in humans that showed trans fats cause heart disease.

—-

Why not?

Because it would have been unethical to test these things in a long-running RCT. People receiving the intervention would have likely been harmed.

Even if it was ethical, the RCT’s would have to run for an incredibly long period of time because diseases like cancer and CVD take decades to develop. It is impractical to run RCT’s that last much longer than 5 years.

So how do we know they are true?

Because of a combination of high quality observational studies (like prospective cohort studies), short term RCT’s that look at biomarkers, and mechanistic studies in animals.

This combination is very powerful and can generate medical knowledge almost as certain as long running randomized controlled trials. We now know with near certainty that smoking causes lung cancer and trans fats cause heart disease, without ever running an RCT to prove it.

Most of the knowledge we have about diet and nutrition was achieved through the same manner as the examples above. Yet you constantly hear criticism that this knowledge is somehow invalid because it was not achieved through long-running RCT’s. This is simply not true, as the history of medicine and the famous examples of smoking and trans fats demonstrate. Are the many studies on smoking and lung cancer invalid because they are not long running RCT’s? Of course not.

Medical textbooks often use smoking and lung cancer as case studies in explaining how to successfully apply the Bradford-Hill criteria for determining whether or not a correlation is causal. They are excellent examples of how to successfully apply these criteria in the real world.

Some of the most reliable knowledge in cardiology was first discovered in the famous Framingham Heart Study— a prospective cohort study— in combination with short term RCTs looking at bio markers, and mechanistic studies in animals.

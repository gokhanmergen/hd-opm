Infections temporarily raise heart attack and stroke risk; vaccines may do so far less

Louis Nervegna · Oct 14, 2024 · ♥ 12 reactions · 0 comments · original Facebook post
Infections & vaccinesHeart attack, stroke & proceduresAspirin, antiplatelets & blood thinners
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that infections such as flu raise short-term heart attack and stroke risk through inflammation and coagulation effects that destabilize plaque, and that the effect fades in a week or two. Vaccines can in theory do this to a much smaller degree. He suggests asking a doctor about a baby aspirin during and shortly after infection, and avoiding hs-CRP tests during that window.

  • Risk rise is temporary and fades after inflammation resolves
  • Vaccine effect would be far smaller, lasting a week or two (per Louis)
  • Louis: ask your doctor about baby aspirin during and 1-2 weeks after infection
  • hs-CRP tests are temporarily inaccurate during infection

Infections temporarily raise the risk of heart attacks and strokes, because of the inflammation that they cause. This is a temporary effect that goes away after the infection is over and the inflammation dies away.

In theory, vaccines can temporarily do the same thing, but to a much lesser degree because the inflammatory response is generally a lot smaller. The temporary effect dissipates in a week or two after the inflammation dissipates.

This is all well known in the medical literature. There is no conspiracy theory to hide it. If you’re worried about it, talk to your doctor. If your doctor approves, it is reasonable to consider taking a baby aspirin during— and for a week or two after— the infection or vaccination. The inflammation generated by an infection or vaccination is generally gone after a week or two.

You should also avoid getting blood tests that check inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP during this period because they will be temporarily very high, and therefore innaccurate.

Shared from Louis Nervegna · original post

Having an infection— like a cold or flu— temporarily raises your risk of having a heart attack or stroke.

This has been known for a long time. Another study confirms it.

If you get sick and have CVD, you may want to talk to your doctor about temporarily taking a baby aspirin every day until you are well again (and for a period afterward), if you don’t already do so.

Quoted:

“Bruijning-Verhagen and colleagues hypothesized that the increased risk of acute MI after acute influenza and other respiratory infections is caused by increased metabolic demand and effects on inflammatory and coagulation pathways that lead to atherosclerotic plaque destabilization and subsequent occlusion.”

NYU researchers developed PRESS, combining platelet aggregation responses and RNA sequencing, to detect platelet hyperreactivity. In PAD patients, hyperreactive platelets predicted more major adverse cardiac or limb events within 30 days of revascularization, and PRESS above the median carried a 90% higher risk of major events over 18 months. It might help target aspirin.

Why fasting insulin is very low on a carnivore diet

Louis Nervegna · Aug 13, 2025 · ♥ 7 reactions · 10 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that high protein and near-zero carbs force insulin very low and glucagon high when fasting, the hormonal setting needed for gluconeogenesis, which can look like pancreatic insufficiency on labs.

  • Very low insulin and high glucagon support hepatic gluconeogenesis

Why does fasting insulin drop so low in people on a carnivore diet?

Often to levels so low that they might be flagged as indicative of pancreatic insufficiency?

My answer:

On a carnivore diet, protein intake is very high and carb intake is near zero.

The body forces insulin very low and glucagon very high in the fasting state on this kind of diet, because this is the only way that gluconeogenesis can occur in the liver. This requires the specific hormonal environment of very low insulin to avoid suppressing hepatic glucose production and elevated glucagon to stimulate the gluconeogenesis.

You have very little stored glycogen on this diet, so the body has no choice but to force gluconeogenesis to occur during fasting or you will become hypoglycemic. This very low level of insulin and high level of glucagon then allows the liver to convert the dietary protein into glucose. That’s why the insulin gets so low in the fasted state in people on this diet.

Abnormally low insulin in this situation is not reflective of better metabolic health. It is reflective of an ancient evolutionary survival mechanism that is kicking-in to keep you alive in an extreme situation.

Once you add back a small amount of carbs, the need for constant gluconeogenesis during fasting disappears and the fasting insulin normalizes to healthy levels again.

Fact checking my previous answer with Claude AI:

(I encourage people to use these tools to do this kind of fact checking before posting anything. This will help to keep the information in the group more accurate, and you will learn a lot in the process.)

You raise an important point about the metabolic adaptations that occur with very high protein, very low carbohydrate diets and how they can create misleading fasting insulin measurements.

Your explanation of the mechanism makes physiological sense. In the absence of dietary carbohydrates and with depleted glycogen stores, the body must maintain glucose homeostasis almost entirely through gluconeogenesis. This process requires a specific hormonal environment - suppressed insulin to prevent interference with hepatic glucose production, and elevated glucagon to stimulate the necessary enzymatic pathways for converting amino acids to glucose.

This creates an interesting clinical scenario where someone might have very low fasting insulin values that could be interpreted as excellent insulin sensitivity, when in reality this reflects metabolic necessity rather than optimal metabolic health. The fasting measurement becomes less meaningful because it's reflecting an adapted survival mechanism rather than normal postprandial insulin dynamics.

The contrast you describe - where reintroducing some carbohydrates allows fasting insulin to normalize - suggests that the extremely low fasting insulin wasn't necessarily indicating superior metabolic health, but rather a compensatory response to dietary macronutrient composition.

This highlights a broader issue with using single biomarkers out of context. Fasting insulin is typically interpreted within the framework of a mixed macronutrient diet, so its diagnostic utility may be compromised in populations following extreme dietary patterns. It would be interesting to see how postprandial insulin responses differ between these dietary approaches, as that might provide a more complete picture of insulin sensitivity and metabolic flexibility.

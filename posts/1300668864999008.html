Statins raise type 2 diabetes risk 9-12% relative, a tiny absolute effect for most people

Louis Nervegna · May 25, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post
StatinsInsulin resistance & diabetes
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that in roughly 5-year RCTs, statins raise relative risk of new type 2 diabetes by 9-12% (20-36% at very high intensity). In absolute terms this is about 1 in 1000 going to 1.12 in 1000, so low-risk people see little change, while people already on the verge of diabetes may be nudged over the threshold sooner.

  • Relative risk +9% to 12% over about 5 years
  • Very high-intensity regimens +20% to 36%
  • 1 in 1000 risk becomes about 1.12 in 1000
  • Matters mainly for those already near the diabetes threshold

Statins and Diabetes
In randomized controlled trials lasting around 5 years, statins increase the relative risk of developing new-onset type 2 diabetes by about 9% to 12%.

The exact increase is dependent on the dosage and intensity of the statin, with very high-intensity regimens pushing the relative risk upward to around 20% to 36%.

Spoiler alert: These numbers are correct. But I purposely stated them in the scariest way possible, to make a point.

What do these numbers actually mean?

Let’s walk through some examples…

If your risk of developing diabetes in the next 5 years is about 1 in 1000, then it means that your risk increases to about 1.12 in 1000.

If you are on very high intensity statins, then it increases to as much as 1.36 in 1000.

This is tiny!

But if you are right on the verge of developing diabetes anyway, then it can push you over the edge.

Say your risk of developing diabetes in the next 5 years is 1 in 2.

Now your risk goes up to 1.12 in 2, or as high as 1.36 in 2 on very high intensity dosing.

This can be enough to nudge you over the official diagnostic threshold.

The point is that people at low risk for diabetes see no appreciable increase in risk from statins.

But people at very high risk of developing diabetes can see enough of an increase in risk to push them over the edge. These people were likely going to develop diabetes anyway. The statin causes it to happen a little bit sooner than it would have otherwise.

Extensive clinical data and major meta-analyses of randomized trials backs this up:

  • The Absolute Risk: Over a 5-year treatment period, this elevated risk translates to only about 1 extra case of diabetes per 1,000 patients treated.

The bottomline is that the small increased risk of new-onset diabetes is considered clinically minor compared to the major cardiovascular benefits that statins provide.

In JUPITER, rosuvastatin brought diabetes diagnosis forward by only 5.4 weeks

Louis uses the JUPITER trial (rosuvastatin 20 mg vs placebo, 17,600 people, up to 5 years) to size the statin-diabetes link. Statin allocation advanced the time to diabetes diagnosis by an average of 5.4 weeks and raised HbA1c about 0.1 point at 24 months. His reading is that no one got diabetes who was not going to anyway, and that 20 mg is a high dose versus the 5 mg many take. The Lancet analysis concluded benefits exceeded the diabetes hazard.

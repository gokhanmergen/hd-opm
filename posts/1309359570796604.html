Lowering IGF1 extends lifespan in mice, so be wary of 'anti-aging' growth hormone claims

Louis Nervegna · Jun 04, 2026 · ♥ 8 reactions · 2 comments · original Facebook post
Aging & longevityHormones, women's & men's health
AI summary & key facts

Louis argues that growth hormone or IGF1 should not be marketed as anti-aging, because lowering them consistently extends lifespan in rodents. The record mouse, a growth hormone receptor knockout, lived almost 5 years versus about 2.5 for a normal lab mouse.

  • GHR-KO mouse lived almost 5 years vs about 2.5 years normal
  • Experiment by Andrzej Bartke, repeated many times in mice and rats

You should be suspicious of claims that growth hormone or IGF1 are “anti-aging” or will extend your lifespan.

Lowering growth hormone or IGF1 in mice and rats consistently extends lifespan, often by very large amounts. This experiment has been repeated many times now.

One mouse lived almost 5 years, which is a world record. A normal lab mouse lives about 2.5 years. Lowering IGF1 doubled its lifespan.

This was a growth hormone receptor knockout mouse, appropriately named GHR-KO 11C. It was genetically engineered in such a way that it was unable to produce much IGF1. The experiment was done by Andrzej Bartke PhD, at Southern Illinois University:

MDLinxOn this day in medical history: Yoda, the oldest living lab mouse, died | MDLinxOn this day in medical history, Yoda the oldest living lab mouse died. He was 4 years and 12 days old, equivalent to 136 human years.mdlinx.com

(Note that there are several long-lived mice with low growth hormone and/or low IGF1 that are referred to in this article. I am specifically referring to the mouse named GHR-KO 11C, not to the one named Yoda. Both mice are mentioned in the article. Yoda was a snell dwarf mouse, who did not live as long as GHR-KO 11C.)

Amazingly, this was in 2003, and the world record still holds to this day.

The liver directly produces IGF1 from growth hormone, in response to activation of growth hormone receptors. For this reason, any intervention that raises growth hormone also raises IGF1. But when these growth hormone receptors are knocked-out, the liver can no longer produce IGF1.

There is a rare human disease called Laron Syndrome, where growth hormone receptors in the liver are naturally knocked-out, just like in GHR-KO mice. As a result, people with Laron Syndrome naturally have very low IGF1.

These people with Laron Syndrome seem to be almost immune to terminal cancer. Diabetes and Alzheimer’s are also extremely uncommon, and they might also have a lower risk of heart disease:

↪ Laron syndrome, with lifelong low IGF-1 and mTOR, shows low cancer, diabetes and CVD risk

↪ People with Laron syndrome (low IGF1) appear to have low cardiovascular risk

An early stage Silicon Valley biotech company called Loyal is developing a longevity drug for dogs that works by lowering IGF1. Although they have not published results yet, they claim that their preliminary data shows that the drug significantly extends dog lifespan:

Proponents of growth hormone for “anti-aging” often cite a small human study called the TRIIM trial, which showed that short term use of growth hormone can modestly reverse epigenetic age as measured by the Horvath clock:

Paper · Aging Cell · 2019Reversal of epigenetic aging and immunosenescent trends in humans.Epigenetic "clocks" can now surpass chronological age in accuracy for estimating biological age. Here, we use four such age estimators to show that epigenetic aging can be reversed in humans. Using a protocol intended to regenerate the thymus, we observed protective immunological changes, improved risk indices for many age-related diseases, and a mean epigenetic age approximately 1.5 years less than baseline after 1…pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

The reversal of epigenetic age in the TRIIM trial was small, 2.5 years.

In hindsight, this is perhaps not all that surprising. We’ve known since at least the 1980’s that short term use of growth hormone can have regenerative properties (and risks). It is long term elevation of growth hormone or IGF1 that consistently shortens lifespan in animal models, not short term use.

Since the publication of the TRIIM trial results, there have been multiple other safer interventions that have produced reversals in epigenetic age of this same magnitude or more.

Steve Horvath himself, the inventor of the epigenetic age test, reversed his own epigenetic age by 4 years, just by taking statins, eating more vegetables, walking, and adding a few common dietary supplements:

↪ Epigenetic clock pioneer Horvath reportedly cut his biological age 4 years in 9 months.

This is much safer than taking growth hormone, and it produced a significantly larger epigenetic age reversal.

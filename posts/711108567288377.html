Plaque regression: about half at LDL near 80, 90% of regressable plaques below 60

AI summary & key facts

Louis shares a Medscape interview on coronary plaque regression, quoting that at an LDL of about 80 mg/dL roughly half of subjects had regression, and below 60 mg/dL about 90% of regressable (lipid-rich) plaques regressed. He adds that combining LDL lowering with targeting inflammation, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction and aging is the future of prevention.

  • LDL ~80 mg/dL: about half of subjects had coronary regression
  • LDL below 60 mg/dL: 90% of regressable lipid-rich plaques regressed
  • Regression is not 'simplistic' per the interview title

There are now lots of documented medical cases of people reversing arterial plaque by simply lowering their LDL-C to around 80 or below.

Quoted from the interview:

“At about an LDL of 80 mg/dL, with everything else being equal, about half of the subjects had coronary artery disease regression. But as you got the LDL below 60 mg/dL, 90% of the regressable plaques or the lipid-rich plaques could show a regression.”

My note: As wonderful as this sounds, it probably still leaves an awful lot on the table. Combining this LDL lowering approach with other interventions that target other causal risk factors like inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction— as well as the aging process itself— is the future of preventative cardiology.

FLOWPROMOTE: combination therapy shrank soft plaque 28% vs 20% with LDL near 42 vs 70

A shared post on FLOWPROMOTE (JAMA Cardiology, Oct 30, 2026): 104 statin-naive people with coronary narrowing were scanned with CT, then given atorvastatin 40 mg or rosuvastatin 40 mg plus ezetimibe 10 mg. After 18 months LDL fell to 42.5 versus 69.5 mg/dL, and soft plaque shrank 28.2% versus 20.0%. Louis shares it with hashtags only.

About 95% of acute-thrombosis heart attacks involve soft plaque, not calcified plaque

Oct 03, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 9 Plaque, CAC & CT angiography

The post says about 64% of heart attacks from acute thrombosis come from rupture of soft lipid-rich plaque and about 25% from erosion of soft plaque, while only about 5% involve calcified nodules. Because statins and other lipid-lowering drugs stabilize and reverse soft plaque, the poster argues they address the plaque type most responsible for heart attacks.

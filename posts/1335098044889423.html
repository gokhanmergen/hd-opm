Gel, feed and sweep: why the word 'fiber' on a label says little about its effect

Louis Nervegna · Jul 04, 2026 · ♥ 35 reactions · 9 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis recommends a Laurie Marbas article arguing that fibers differ in what they do in the body. The shared excerpt describes a 1978 experiment where the same fiber blunted a glucose spike only while it formed a thick gel, and proposes thinking of gel, feed and sweep fibers rather than soluble versus insoluble.

  • 1978 experiment: same fiber, same dose; blood sugar barely moved when the fiber was thick and gelled, but spiked once its gel structure was broken down
  • Proposed framework: GEL fibers (e.g. oats) lower cholesterol and steady blood sugar; FEED fibers (beans, onions) feed gut bacteria; SWEEP fibers (flax, veggie skins) keep things moving

Shared from Dr. Laurie Marbas · original post

Around 1978, researchers had volunteers drink liquid glucose mixed with a thick fiber that turned it into something like warm pudding. Their blood sugar barely moved. Then they took the same fiber, broke down its structure so it was no longer thick, and ran it again. This time the blood sugar spiked as if the fiber wasn't even there.

Same fiber, same dose, same people. The only thing that changed was the thickness of the gel.

Here's what that means for you. The word "fiber" on a label tells you almost nothing about what it actually does inside you. Two fibers can both say "soluble," sit on the same line of the same panel, and have completely opposite effects on your cholesterol, blood sugar, and gut.

So forget soluble versus insoluble. Think of your gut as an orchestra with three sections. GEL fibers like oats gel up to lower cholesterol and steady blood sugar. FEED fibers like beans and onions feed your gut bacteria. SWEEP fibers like flax and veggie skins keep things moving. Health comes from all three playing, not from maxing out one.

Louis quotes a pharmacist's simple test: psyllium forms a thick gel in water while inulin dissolves clear. Only the gel-forming fibers trap bile acids, making the liver pull cholesterol from the blood, so for LDL choose psyllium or oat/barley beta-glucan, while inulin is a fine prebiotic.

Psyllium lowers LDL about 13 mg/dL across 28 trials by trapping bile acids

Oct 31, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 15 · 💬 4 Carbs, fiber, sugar & whole grains

A shared explainer says psyllium lowers LDL by about 13 mg/dL across 28 randomized trials, and that the mechanism is mechanical: its viscous gel traps bile acids so the liver pulls cholesterol from the blood to make more, upregulating LDL receptors. It does not absorb cholesterol directly or scrub the gut, which is why most other soluble fibers do not work the same way.

Louis quotes a pharmacist's rule that psyllium's gel can slow absorption of other pills, so doses should be separated by about 2 hours, with extra care for thyroid drugs and narrow-window medications. Adding psyllium to a statin reportedly lowered LDL about as much as doubling the statin dose.

Louis shares a summary of a 2022 meta-analysis of 74 randomized trials of oat consumption. Oats versus non-oat controls lowered LDL-C by 0.29 mmol/L, total cholesterol by 0.42 mmol/L, blood sugar, weight and waist, though apoB did not change significantly in just 2 studies. He recommends slow-cook steel-cut oats over instant oats.

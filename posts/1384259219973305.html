Vitamin K2 MK-7 slowed coronary calcification in a 2-year RCT; soft plaque not measured

Louis Nervegna · Sep 01, 2026 · ♥ 12 reactions · 11 comments · original Facebook post
Supplements & vitaminsPlaque, CAC & CT angiography
AI summary & key facts

A shared post on a new 2-year randomized trial notes that MK-7 reduced progression of coronary calcification versus placebo. It cautions that calcified plaque is generally more stable, soft plaque was not measured, and the trial was not designed to show fewer cardiovascular events.

  • 2-year randomized trial of vitamin K2 MK-7 vs placebo
  • Less coronary calcification progression with MK-7
  • Soft plaque not measured; no event data

Shared from Physionic · original post

A new, larger randomized trial just put vitamin K2 back in the spotlight for arterial calcification.

Vitamin K2 activates Matrix Gla Protein (MGP), which helps inhibit the formation and growth of calcium crystals within the arterial wall. That makes it biologically plausible that vitamin K2 could slow coronary artery calcification.

And in this new 2-year randomized trial, vitamin K2 MK-7 did exactly that: people supplementing with vitamin K2 experienced less progression of coronary artery calcification than those receiving placebo.

That sounds great—but there’s an important catch.

More coronary artery calcium is associated with greater cardiovascular disease risk, but calcified plaque itself is generally considered more stable than softer forms of plaque.

So simply reducing calcification doesn’t automatically mean you’re reducing cardiovascular risk.

The study did NOT measure soft plaque. This leaves open an important question: did vitamin K2 reduce the overall progression of atherosclerosis, or did it simply alter how much of the plaque became calcified?

It also wasn’t designed to determine whether vitamin K2 reduces heart attacks or other cardiovascular events.

So, this study moves me slightly more toward believing that higher-dose vitamin K2 MK-7 can slow arterial calcification—particularly in people who already have coronary artery disease—but I’m still not convinced that reducing calcification itself is necessarily beneficial.

The evidence remains mixed, and what we really need is a large randomized trial examining vitamin K2 against actual cardiovascular events.

Review of vitamin K2 (with or without vitamin D) and CAC. A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (n=1,533) found a -17.3 Agatston units/year pooled difference. The largest RCT (389 men, 720 ug MK-7 + 1000 IU D vs placebo for 2 years) missed significance overall but slowed CAC at baseline CAC >=400; some CKD trials were null. Not yet a standard therapy.

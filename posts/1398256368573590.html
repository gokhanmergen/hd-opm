Human trials: omega-6 and oxidized fish oil do not raise oxidation or inflammation markers

Louis Nervegna · Sep 18, 2026 · ♥ 7 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post
Fats & oils (saturated fat, seed oils, omega-6, olive oil)Myths, misinformation & controversiesSupplements & vitamins
AI summary & key facts

The post states that human randomized trials have repeatedly shown omega-6 fats do not cause oxidation or lipid peroxidation, and that oxidized fish oil does not raise inflammation, oxidation or peroxidation markers or worsen cardiometabolic or liver health. It shares a saturated fat versus omega-6 breakdown by Dr Alan Flanagan.

  • Omega-6 does not increase oxidation or peroxidation in human RCTs
  • Oxidized fish oil does not raise markers of inflammation, oxidation or peroxidation
  • No worsening of cardiometabolic or liver markers

The claim that omega 6 fats cause oxidation and lipid peroxidation in the human body is false.

This claim has been tested over and over again in human randomized trials, and it has been repeatedly shown to be wrong.

The same is true with omega 3 fats. Randomized trials demonstrate that oxidized fish oil does not increase markers of inflammation, oxidation, or peroxidation in humans: facebook.com/share/v/1DVrhBfp9K. Nor does it make markers of cardiometabolic or liver health worse.

Olive oil has the strongest trial evidence, but canola and soybean oil also have support

Nov 10, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 2 Fats & oils (saturated fat, seed oils, omega-6, olive oil)

Louis explains that olive oil is the only fat tested in multiple large randomized outcome trials (CORDIOPREV, PREDIMED, Lyon). Lyon also tested canola oil with olive oil and saw the largest total mortality reduction in a cardiology prevention trial, and the Indo-Mediterranean study (about 1,000 people) tested soybean oil. Harvard cohorts link canola or soybean oil in place of butter to lower cancer, CV and total mortality.

100+ feeding trials: polyunsaturated fat improves glucose control, saturated fat does not

Louis summarizes the 2016 PLoS Medicine meta-analysis of randomized feeding trials: polyunsaturated fat, mainly omega-6 from vegetable oils, lowered fasting glucose and A1C and improved insulin resistance and secretion, MUFA did well but less so, and saturated fat showed no appreciable benefit. He pairs it with the 20-cohort biomarker study showing a 43% lower diabetes risk with high omega-6.

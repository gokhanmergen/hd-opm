Centenarians' higher LDL is survivorship bias, not evidence that high LDL is protective

Why do studies show that centenarians tend to have have higher LDL?

Because studies of centenarians suffer from what epidemiologists call “survivorship bias”:

The reason centenarians have higher LDL is the same reason that the general population has higher LDL— namely, higher LDL is very common. Most people who follow a typical western diet and lifestyle will have higher LDL, including centenarians. Some lucky people posses genes that strongly protect them against atherosclerosis and other major diseases, and these people tend to live a long time. They don’t get atherosclerosis, despite having higher LDL. These genes are common in centenarians. So the centenarians are not living long because of higher LDL— they are living long in spite of it— unlike the rest of us normal people who would die much younger with higher LDL and never make it to 100. This is an example of survivorship bias.

Naturally low LDL is very uncommon in western countries. Studies have shown that the main reason why people in western countries live past 100 is genetics. Genetics is the primary determinant of who ultimately becomes a centenarian in western countries, not diet or lifestyle.

Low LDL by itself is not going to get you anywhere near 100. It may protect you from dying of CVD, or at least delay it— but something else will likely kill you a few years later anyway. For example, having naturally low LDL will do little, if anything, to protect you against cancer.

So chances are very high that centenarians in western countries are not going to have low LDL. It is very uncommon in western countries to begin with, and it is not going to play a major role in getting them to 100 anyway. So it is unlikely they would have it.

This is survivorship bias in a nutshell. This is why centenarians tend to have higher LDL, just like everyone else.

There are hundreds of prospective studies that show a U-shaped curve with LDL and mortality. High LDL is associated with a very high increase in mortality in these studies (the right side of the U). Mendelian randomization studies consistently show that people born with genes for lower LDL live longer. Multiple well done studies also show that people with genetic hypercholesterolemia (very high LDL from birth) have heart attacks and strokes at a young age and die much sooner than average. The very high LDL is shortening their lives, not lengthening it.

The classic example of survivorship bias is planes with bullet holes that survived in WW2. Originally the air force was planning to add additional armor to all the places where they observed bullet holes in planes that survived. This seemed to make sense.

But then a very smart statistician stepped in and said, NO! Put the extra armor in all the places that do NOT have bullet holes!

The statistician was right.

They saw the bullet holes where they did because the planes that survived were already protected from lethal damage where the bullets hit, either because the armor was already sufficient in these locations or because the locations were not critical.

Concentrating on where the holes were located in the surviving planes while ignoring the location of the holes in the destroyed planes is a classic example of “survivorship bias”.

The planes that did NOT survive lacked sufficient protection in the other places where they were hit. So the correct solution was to protect the other places.

It is the same phenomenon with LDL. High LDL is the norm for almost everyone in western countries. The ones who survive to 100 are lucky enough to be genetically protected against it. The vast majority who die are missing that genetic protection. Just like with the planes, the correct solution is to add additional “armor” to the vulnerable places— the lack of protective genes— to mimic the protection that the survivors received. That “armor” is cholesterol lowering drugs.

Victorian-diet longevity claim is survivorship bias from excluding deaths under age 5

Louis uses a paper on mid-Victorian diets as an example of survivorship bias. The authors exclude deaths before age 5, but those who survive early childhood were genetically selected for health, so their long lives do not show that the diet made average adults live as long as people today. Including the weaker people would have produced a much lower average age of death. The linked paper itself says life expectancy at age 5 was as good or better than today.

Daily tomatoes and tomato sauce reduced liver fat in fatty liver without weight loss

Louis Nervegna · Aug 20, 2026 · ♥ 15 reactions · 5 comments · original Facebook post
Weight, visceral & liver fatNuts, fish, coffee, tea, cocoa & other foods
AI summary & key facts

In a 6-week randomized trial of 79 adults with MASLD, eating 200 g raw tomatoes plus 50 g tomato sauce daily lowered FibroScan liver fat more than a tomato-free diet. Body weight, fat mass, waist and metabolic markers did not differ, suggesting a food effect independent of weight loss. The trial is small and short.

  • 79 adults with MASLD and BMI <=30, randomized 6 weeks
  • 200 g raw tomatoes + 50 g tomato sauce daily vs tomato-free diet
  • Liver fat (CAP) change: -35 vs -18 dB/m
  • No significant difference in weight, BMI, waist, fat mass, liver stiffness or lipids

Conventional wisdom is that you need to lose weight in order to reduce liver fat… and this certainly works.

But more and more research is pointing to the fact that specific foods can reduce liver fat, irrespective of weight loss.

In this small randomized trial, tomatoes and tomato sauce reduced liver fat, without any accompanying weight loss. The changes were measured by fibroscan (elastography ultrasound), which is very accurate for assessing fat content of the liver.

PaperMDPI articlemdpi.com

Quoted:

These findings suggest that tomato consumption may reduce hepatic fat accumulation independently of major changes in body weight, total and abdominal adiposity, [and] support the potential role of tomato-based interventions as part of nutritional management for MASLD [fatty liver],” the authors of the study wrote.

The researchers randomized 79 adults (median age, about 58 years; about 70% men) with MASLD [fatty liver] and a BMI ≤ 30 to a tomato-rich diet (42 participants) or a tomato-free diet (37 participants) for 6 weeks.

The intervention group ate 200 g of raw tomatoes and 50 g of tomato sauce daily; control individuals did not eat tomatoes in any form.

The researchers measured liver fat using the FibroScan-based controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) at baseline and follow-up.

Secondary outcomes included DEXA-measured body composition, liver stiffness, and metabolic and biochemical markers.

CAP [liver fat] decreased in both groups, with a significantly greater reduction in the intervention group (median change: -35.00 vs -18.00 dB/m), a difference that held after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, and at follow-up.

Hepatic steatosis reduction occurred without measurable changes in body composition or most metabolic and biochemical markers.

At the trial end, body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and DEXA-measured fat mass did not differ significantly between groups.

Liver stiffness, fibrosis scores, and metabolic markers such as glucose, lipids, and liver enzymes also did not differ significantly between groups.

Dehydration after 65 raises risk of falls, fractures and delirium as thirst fades

Shared from Eric Verdin · original post

As the weather gets warmer, here is one of the simplest things you can do for your health:

Drink more water.

I know. Not exactly the longevity intervention you’d expect from a scientist who studies aging biology for a living. But it matters more than most people realize.

Dehydration is a major risk factor for death in older adults. After 65, chronic dehydration meaningfully raises your risk of falls, fractures, and delirium.

Part of the problem is that the body stops warning you. Your thirst mechanism declines with age. The brain circuitry that signals you to drink becomes less sensitive over time, so older adults genuinely feel less thirsty even when their body needs fluid.

Your kidneys also become less efficient. They lose some of their ability to concentrate urine, which means the body is worse at conserving water exactly when it needs to.

In other words, the signal telling you to drink gets quieter just as your margin for error gets smaller.

And the downstream effects of chronic mild dehydration are not trivial. It impairs cellular function across every system, accelerates skin aging, raises diabetes risk, and contributes to cognitive decline and delirium.

The takeaway: older adults can’t rely on thirst as a reliable signal. Drink consistently, before you feel the need.

Especially as summer arrives.

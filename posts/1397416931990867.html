Halving dietary protein for 4 weeks did not increase autophagy in human immune cells

Louis Nervegna · Sep 17, 2026 · ♥ 15 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post
ProteinAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

In a randomized crossover trial of healthy adults, reducing protein from 20% to 10% of calories for four weeks did not significantly change autophagic flux in blood immune cells. Louis concludes that moderate protein restriction alone, without fasting or calorie restriction, does not appear to raise basal autophagy. Caveats include measurement only in circulating immune cells and a short duration; this is a preprint.

  • Randomized crossover design, 4-week diets with 4-week washout
  • Average-protein 20% vs reduced-protein 10% of energy, calories balanced
  • Primary outcome: autophagic flux in whole-blood PBMCs
  • Confidence interval crossed zero; no significant effect
  • Preprint (medRxiv), not yet peer reviewed

Cutting protein intake in half (from 20% to 10% of calories) did not significantly activate autophagy in human immune cells, over a 4 week trial period.

Moderate protein restriction alone, in the absence of fasting or caloric restriction, does not seem to increase basal levels of autophagy in circulating immune cells of healthy adults.

“We conducted a randomized crossover trial in which 74 healthy adults were randomized to receive two 4-week interventions of either average-protein (20% energy) or reduced-protein (10% energy) diets prescribed to maintain calculated energy balance, separated by a 4-week washout period. Shared from William Wallace, Ph.D. · original post

A common longevity claim is that lowering protein automatically “turns on” autophagy. A new human trial tested that assumption much more directly than most of the evidence behind it.

Researchers enrolled 63 healthy adults in a randomized crossover study. Each person completed two four-week diets: one providing about 20% of calories from protein and another providing about 10%. Total calorie intake was kept similar, allowing the researchers to isolate protein reduction rather than the effects of fasting or weight loss.

Importantly, they did not rely on a single static autophagy marker. They measured autophagic flux, which tracks how quickly cellular material is actually being processed through the autophagy pathway in circulating immune cells.

Cutting protein intake in half did not significantly increase that flux. The estimated difference favored neither diet clearly, and the confidence interval crossed zero.

That does not mean protein intake has no relationship with autophagy. Amino acids, particularly leucine, interact with mTOR signaling, and animal and cell studies provide strong mechanistic reasons to expect nutrient availability to influence the pathway. But mechanism is not the same as demonstrating that moderate protein restriction increases autophagy in humans.
There are also important boundaries. This study measured blood immune cells, not liver, skeletal muscle, or brain. It lasted four weeks per diet. And calories were maintained, so it tells us little about fasting or caloric restriction, where the biology may be different.

Simply cutting protein from about 20% to 10% of calories was not enough to produce a measurable increase in autophagic flux in this human study.

That makes “eat less protein to activate autophagy” a much less settled recommendation than it is often presented.

Reference:

Singh S, Fourrier C, Hein LK, et al. “Reduced dietary protein intake does not alter autophagy in human blood: A randomized crossover study in healthy adults.”

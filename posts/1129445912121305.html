Admin prefers recent, local epidemiology because food supply and pesticides change

Gokhan Can · Oct 06, 2025 · ♥ 15 reactions · 6 comments · original Facebook post
How to read researchEnvironment (microplastics, pollution, heat)
AI summary & key facts

Opinion that foods change over time so he favors recent US epidemiology over old or foreign studies. No research cited.

The foods we're eating today are different from those found 10, 20 years ago. They might look the same but in reality toxins in them are regularly being updated (thanks to novel pesticide coctails). Therefore I like to pay attention to recent and local (USA) epidemiology, not to a study that was done 50 years ago or in a separate country.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: weighing unproven interventions

Oct 30, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 6 · 💬 1 How to read research

Louis explains that an intervention that has not been properly studied is indeterminate, not proven ineffective, and that this differs from interventions proven not to work. For speculative items such as supplements, he suggests assigning a subjective probability from limited evidence, while warning against betting your life on indeterminate interventions.

Randomized trials and prospective cohorts top the evidence hierarchy

Sep 18, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 18 · 💬 2 How to read research

The poster explains that randomized controlled trials can prove causality and prospective cohort studies track long-term outcomes, so the two together are the best evidence. He warns that people often cite lower-level studies to refute higher-level ones and urges readers to ask where a study sits on the hierarchy.

Open-label placebo beat deceptive placebo in 90 older adults in a small trial

A Nautilus article describes a trial of 90 healthy adults aged 65+ split into deceptive placebo, open-label placebo and control groups for three weeks. The open-label group, told they were taking sugar pills, showed less stress and better memory and physical performance than the deceptive group.

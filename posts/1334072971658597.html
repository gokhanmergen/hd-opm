90-120 min/week of strength training linked to 13% lower mortality, 19% fewer CVD deaths

Louis Nervegna · Jul 03, 2026 · ♥ 11 reactions · 3 comments · original Facebook post
Exercise & fitnessCardiovascular disease: the big pictureAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

Louis summarizes new results from the Harvard Nurses' Health Studies and Health Professionals Follow-up Study, following about 147,000 people for 30 years. Roughly 90-120 minutes a week of strength training was tied to lower all-cause, cardiovascular and neurological mortality, with the largest gains when combined with plenty of aerobic exercise.

  • Over 147,000 participants followed for 30 years (Nurses' Health Study 1 & 2, Health Professionals Follow-up Study)
  • 90-120 min/week strength training: 13% lower all-cause mortality, 19% fewer cardiovascular deaths, 27% fewer neurological deaths; no added benefit beyond 120 min
  • Cancer mortality: 21% lower risk at 1-30 min/week and 18% at 30-60 min, with the benefit disappearing beyond 60 min
  • Aerobic activity above 7.5 MET-hours/week: 26-43% lower risk of death
  • Strength training combined with high aerobic volume: 45-58% lower total mortality

A 30-year prospective study of over 147,000 participants found that 90–120 minutes of weekly strength training was linked to major longevity benefits, especially as related to cardiovascular disease and dementia. And especially when combined with aerobic exercise.

These are the latest results from the Harvard Nurses Study 1 & 2 and the Harvard Physicians Followup Study.

Participants were tracked prospectively all the way from midlife to old age and death, making this one of the longest and highest-quality prospective studies to ever track the effects of exercise in humans.

Strength training of 90-120 minute duration was linked to 13% lower all-cause mortality, 19% fewer cardiovascular deaths and 27% reduced neurological fatalities from diseases such as Alzheimer’s. There was no added benefit beyond 120 minutes.

Cancer mortality benefits emerged at even lower volumes, with just 1-30 minutes weekly tied to a 21% risk reduction, and 30-60 minutes tied to an 18% reduction. Strangely, the cancer benefit disappeared beyond 60 minutes per week. (The authors speculate that this disappearance might be due to increases in systemic levels of IGF1.)

Aerobic exercise on its own also showed strong benefits. Any amount above 7.5 MET hours per week (a little under 2 hours of brisk walking) was associated with a 26-43% lower risk of death.

Combining resistance sessions with high levels of aerobic exercise produced the greatest gains—huge risk reductions in total mortality from 45% to 58%.

These huge 45% to 58% mortality drops occurred at high volumes of cardio. The 45% to 53% reduction was seen in individuals combining 60–120 minutes of strength training with 30–44 MET-hours/week of aerobic activity (roughly 7 to 10 hours of brisk walking weekly). The 53% to 58% reduction required over 45 MET-hours/week of cardio (equivalent to briskly walking 11–15 hours a week).

Women in particular saw a major benefit in this study. Those who performed at least 2 hours (120 minutes) of resistance training per week saw a 20% lower risk of major heart disease and a 44% lower risk of heart attacks.

The caveat: As with most observational studies on exercise, reverse causality likely played a major role here. Healthy people who feel good are more likely to possess the physical capacity and motivation to perform strength training and aerobic exercise in the first place. It is impossible to know how much of the benefit in this study was due to forward vs reverse causality. The results are likely a combination of both.

Quoted:

The study looked at three decades’ worth of data from nearly 150,000 adults participating in three long-running studies—the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study, the Nurses’ Health Study, and the Nurses’ Health Study II. Researchers tracked participants’ weekly resistance training and aerobic exercise duration via periodic questionnaires.

Adults who averaged 90 and 120 minutes of weekly resistance training—activities such as pushups, squats, lunges, or weightlifting—had a 13% lower risk of early death from any cause, a 19% lower risk from heart disease, and a 27% lower risk from neurological diseases like Alzheimer’s, compared with those who did no strength training. Doing strength training more than 120 minutes a week did not appear to provide any additional benefit, the study found.

The study also found that participants who did both strength and aerobic training, such as walking, running, swimming, cycling, or tennis, had among the lowest mortality risks in the study—up to 45% lower risk than those who didn’t do either kind of training.

Arterial calcification reversal is possible in principle but speculative in practice

Louis Nervegna · Jul 11, 2026 · ♥ 20 reactions · 6 comments · original Facebook post
Plaque, CAC & CT angiographyBlood vessels, circulation & endothelium
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that soft tissue calcification is an actively regulated process that can sometimes be reversed, for example in calcific tendonitis, through macrophage-driven dissolving. Whether artery calcification can be reversed is poorly understood.

  • Macrophages can act like osteoclasts to dissolve calcium
  • Calcific tendonitis is a well-known spontaneous example
  • Methods for arteries are speculative

Is it possible to reverse calcification of soft tissue?
Yes, there are documented examples where reversal of soft tissue calcification occurs in the human body.

Does this mean that it is also possible to reverse calcification of arteries?

In principle, yes.

But in practice, the methods by which this could be accomplished are highly speculative and poorly understood.

For a long time, medical science viewed tissue calcification as a passive, irreversible one-way street— essentially just an unavoidable byproduct of aging or cellular death where calcium salts precipitated out of the blood.

However, modern research has flipped this concept on its head. We now know that ectopic calcification (calcification outside of bone) is a highly regulated, active biological process that heavily mirrors bone formation.

Because it is actively managed by cells, it can also be actively reversed.

How It Works in Principle

The body clears calcification using cellular mechanisms that mimic how bone is broken down and remodeled:

  • Macrophage Transdifferentiation: White blood cells called macrophages can infiltrate calcified tissue and alter their behavior to act like osteoclasts (the specialized cells that dissolve bone mineral).
  • Acidification and Phagocytosis: These cells secrete acids to dissolve the solid calcium hydroxyapatite crystals back into soluble calcium and phosphate ions, and physically engulf smaller mineral fragments to clear them from the tissue.

Documented Examples of Regression

While it is difficult to achieve in chronic diseases like advanced coronary artery disease (where the body's anti-calcification defenses are overwhelmed), there are several well-documented clinical examples where calcification spontaneously regresses or completely disappears.

  1. Calcific Tendonitis (e.g., Shoulder Rotator Cuff)

This is the most common real-world example of spontaneous calcification removal. In this condition, calcium deposits inexplicably form inside a tendon.

The Resorptive Phase: After a dormant period, the body suddenly triggers an intense inflammatory response. Macrophages and new micro-blood vessels flood the area, turning the chalky calcium deposit into a toothpaste-like consistency before completely resorbing it. X-rays taken months apart frequently show large calcium deposits entirely vanishing.

  1. Generalized Arterial Calcification of Infancy (GACI)

GACI is a rare, severe genetic disorder where infants are born with widespread, dangerous calcium deposits throughout their large and medium arteries.

The Resolution: While historically fatal, clinical tracking of survivors has yielded incredible insights. Doctors have repeatedly documented cases where these massive, life-threatening arterial calcifications completely regress and resolve over time—sometimes spontaneously, and sometimes accelerated by medications like bisphosphonates (which alter calcium remodeling).

  1. Calcinosis Cutis (Skin and Soft Tissue)

Often seen in patients with autoimmune disorders like systemic sclerosis or lupus, calcium nodules can form under the skin. There are numerous documented medical case studies where balancing the underlying disease, modifying local tissue pH, or spontaneous immune shifts have caused these hard nodules to soften, break down, and be entirely reabsorbed by the surrounding tissue.

  1. Regression of Vascular Calcification in Kidney Patients

In patients with chronic kidney disease, severe mineral imbalances cause heavy calcification of the blood vessels. However, clinical studies have shown that when these patients receive a successful kidney transplant—restoring the body's natural biochemical balance—the progression of vascular calcification can not only halt, but in some patients, significantly regress over subsequent years.

The Takeaway

The biological machinery required to dissolve and remove tissue calcification exists in all of us. The current frontier of cardiovascular medicine isn't trying to invent a way to dissolve calcium, but rather figuring out how to safely turn on these natural cellular cleanup mechanisms in deep arterial walls without disrupting our actual skeletons.

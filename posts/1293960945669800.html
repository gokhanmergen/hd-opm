Master athletes at 63 keep over 80% of peak performance; average people lose 30-48%

Louis Nervegna · May 17, 2026 · ♥ 12 reactions · 2 comments · original Facebook post
Exercise & fitnessAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

William Wallace shares a 47-year Swedish study of 427 people followed from age 16 to 63. Leg power peaks in the late teens to twenties, aerobic capacity in the late twenties to early thirties; by 63 the average person lost 30-48% of peak while master athletes held over 80%, with physical activity the main difference.

  • 427 people followed 47 years, age 16 to 63
  • Average loss of 30-48% of peak by 63
  • Master athletes retained over 80%

Shared from William Wallace, Ph.D. · original post

Physical capacity doesn't necessarily peak at one age. It probably peaks in different windows years apart.

  • Leg power: late teens to late twenties.
  • Aerobic capacity: late twenties to early thirties.
  • Upper-body endurance: mid-thirties.

A 47-year Swedish study followed the same 427 people from age 16 to 63. By 63, the average person had lost 30 to 48% of their peak. Master athletes the same age held over 80%. Physical activity was the primary difference maker (obviously)

Mechanical stress on bones made mice live nearly 3 months longer via anti-aging signals

Nov 03, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 6 Exercise & fitness

A news report on a mouse study found that mechanical strain on the skeleton, similar to what occurs in exercise and resistance training, makes osteocytes secrete anti-aging chemicals. Gently squeezing leg bones extended mouse lifespan by nearly 3 months, roughly nine human years. A related study found bone stress improved memory and motor function after traumatic brain injury in mice and pigs.

Exercise-trained older adults' muscle looked younger at the molecular level

Sep 18, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 9 · 💬 1 Exercise & fitness

A Nature Aging multi-omics study compared skeletal muscle from younger and older adults and found that about half of the age-related molecular differences were absent in exercise-trained older adults, who looked more like young muscle. Gene expression for cellular respiration and energy metabolism was preserved.

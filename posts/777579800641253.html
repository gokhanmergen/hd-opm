CORDIOPREV: Mediterranean diet shrank carotid plaque and cut recurrent events about 25%

Louis Nervegna · Mar 28, 2024 · ♥ 17 reactions · 13 comments · original Facebook post
Mediterranean, DASH, MIND & plant-forward dietsPlaque, CAC & CT angiographyFats & oils (saturated fat, seed oils, omega-6, olive oil)
AI summary & key facts

Louis summarizes CORDIOPREV, an RCT of 1002 coronary patients comparing a Mediterranean diet rich in extra virgin olive oil with a low-fat diet. The Mediterranean diet reduced carotid intima-media thickness at 5 and 7 years while the low-fat diet showed no change, and also lowered carotid plaque height. He also cites roughly 25% fewer recurrent major cardiovascular events over 7 years, with benefits seen despite statin treatment and mean LDL-C below 90.

  • 1002 secondary-prevention patients; Mediterranean (EVOO, fruit, vegetables, whole grains, fish, nuts) vs low-fat diet
  • IMT-CC change: -0.027 mm at 5 years and -0.031 mm at 7 years (both P<.001) on the Mediterranean diet; none on low-fat
  • About 25% lower risk of recurrent MACE over 7 years vs low-fat
  • All patients on standard-of-care drugs; average LDL-C below 90 at baseline

The CORDIOPREV randomized controlled trial, published in 2021, demonstrated that a traditional Mediterranean diet can reverse atherosclerotic plaque in patients that have already had a heart attack or stroke (secondary prevention).

The diet used in this study was rich in extra virgin olive oil, fruit and vegetables, whole grains, fatty fish, and nuts. It encouraged mild to moderate consumption of red wine.

Processed meat and red meat were strongly discouraged.

The Mediterranean diet significantly decreased artery thickness both after 5 years and after 7 years. In contrast, the lower-fat control diet did not exert any change on thickness of the carotid artery.

The Mediterranean diet also produced a greater decrease in arterial plaque maximum height, compared with the lower-fat diet throughout follow-up.

Patients on the traditional Mediterranean diet had roughly a 25% lower risk of recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events, a composite endpoint that included MI, revascularization, ischemic stroke, PAD, and cardiovascular mortality, compared to those who stuck with a reduced-fat approach during 7 years of follow-up.

Note that all patients in this study received standard-of-care medical treatment in combination with the Mediterranean or lower-fat diets, which included medications for blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, etc. The traditional Mediterranean diet produced all of these extra benefits, even though the participants were already on statins and had an average LDL-C < 90 before the study even started.

Quoted:

“The Coronary Diet Intervention With Olive Oil and Cardiovascular Prevention (CORDIOPREV) study is an ongoing prospective study [RCT] comparing the effects of two healthy diets for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in 1002 patients.

The comparative effect of the diets in reducing CVD risk, assessed by quantification of intima-media thickness of the common carotid arteries (IMT-CC), is a key secondary endpoint of the study.

During the study, half of the patients follow a Mediterranean diet rich in EVOO, fruit and vegetables, whole grains, fish, and nuts. The other half follow a diet low in fat and rich in complex carbohydrates.

A total of 939 participants (459 in the low-fat diet group and 480 in the Mediterranean diet group) completed IMT-CC evaluation at baseline, and 809 (377 and 432, respectively) completed the IMT-CC evaluation at 5 years; 731 (335 and 396, respectively) did so at 7 years.

The Mediterranean diet significantly decreased IMT-CC both after 5 years (-0.027; P < .001) and after 7 years (-0.031 mm; P < .001), relative to baseline. In contrast, the low-fat diet did not exert any change on IMT-CC after 5 or 7 years, the researchers report.

The higher the IMT-CC at baseline, the greater the reduction in this parameter.

The Mediterranean diet also produced a greater decrease in IMT-CC and carotid plaque maximum height, compared with the low-fat diet throughout follow-up.”

🧠A commonly taken vitamin could help slow ageing in the brain.

📉New research has suggested that low levels of the vitamin are linked to cognitive decline.

🇯🇵 The study, which was published in PLOS One journal, found that out of more than 2,000 elderly adults in Japan, those with reduced vitamin C levels in their blood appeared to have diminished grey matter and weaker links within a crucial brain network responsible for memory and attention.

As reported by Science Daily, while the study doesn't definitively prove vitamin C safeguards the brain, it reinforces growing evidence that proper nutrition could be vital in preserving cognitive health during later years.

Previous research has indicated that people who consume higher amounts of vitamin C face reduced risks of cognitive decline as they age. However, comparatively limited research has explored whether vitamin C concentrations measured directly in the bloodstream correlate with physical alterations in the brain.

To explore this issue, scientists analysed magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans and blood plasma specimens from 2,044 Japanese adults aged over 64. Utilising the MRI scans, they calculated the volume of grey matter and white matter in each participant's brain while controlling for variations in overall brain size.

They also assessed connectivity within the default mode network, a collection of interconnected brain regions that performs a significant role in attention, autobiographical memory, and other cognitive functions.

Having accounted for other variables known to influence brain health, such as age, educational background and levels of physical activity, researchers identified a recurring pattern. Those participants with lower levels of plasma vitamin C tended to exhibit reduced grey matter volume and diminished connectivity within the default mode network.

The findings indicate that sustaining adequate vitamin C levels could potentially assist in supporting cognitive function and healthy brain ageing. However, the researchers stress that this was an observational study, meaning it is unable to establish whether vitamin C directly causes these variations in brain structure or function.

Study author Tomohiro Shintaku said: "Our study demonstrates that higher plasma vitamin C levels are associated with better preserved structural connectivity of the default mode network (DMN), a key brain network involved in cognitive function. This finding generates the exciting hypothesis that a diet rich in vitamin C might play a supportive role in maintaining brain health and mitigating age-related cognitive decline in older adults."

He added: "What I found most fascinating about this research is that we were able to detect these subtle but significant associations between a single nutritional factor and large-scale brain networks by utilising a robust, community-based cohort of over 2,000 older adults. It truly highlights the potential impact of our everyday dietary habits on our brain structures."

However, the team noted that further research will be required to shed light on the biological mechanisms underpinning these statistical links.

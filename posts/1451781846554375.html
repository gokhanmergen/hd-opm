STREAM: why 'noninferior' does not make stopping statins at age 70 safe

Louis Nervegna · Nov 15, 2026 · ♥ 4 reactions · 11 comments · original Facebook post
StatinsGuidelines, policy & public healthAging & longevity
AI summary & key facts

Louis shares a cardiologist's video commentary on the STREAM randomized trial of stopping statins after age 70. The commentary stresses that the stop group had roughly 38% more events in relative terms (12.7% vs 9.2%), that noninferiority only means the difference fell within a preset margin, and that the safety board advised discussing restarting. Stopping can be appropriate based on frailty or side effects, but not simply because of age.

  • 1,880 adults aged 70+ without known CVD randomized to continue or stop statins
  • Events: 12.7% (stopped) vs 9.2% (continued), about 38% more in relative terms
  • No meaningful improvement in muscle pain, quality of life, falls or sarcopenia
  • Data and safety monitoring board recommended discussing restarting statins

He is commenting on the recent STREAM randomized trial, which tested whether or not it was safe for patients older than 70yo to stop taking statins.

Quoted from the original post:

Stopping statins after 70 is SAFE? I’m not buying that conclusion so easily.

A new study presented at AHA 2026 and published in the New England Journal of Medicine asked a simple question: Can older adults safely stop taking their statins?

The STREAM trial randomized 1,880 adults aged 70 and older, all without known cardiovascular disease, to continue or discontinue their statins.

Here’s what happened:

🔴 Stopped statins: 12.7% experienced a heart attack, ischemic stroke, or death.

🟢 Continued statins: 9.2% experienced those outcomes.

That’s approximately 38% more events in relative terms among those assigned to stop their statins.

Yet the study concluded that stopping was noninferior.

How? Because the trial was designed with a predefined margin of acceptable difference. And statistically, it met that margin.

But here’s what bothers me.

There was no meaningful improvement in muscle pain, quality of life, falls, or sarcopenia.

And the independent data and safety monitoring board ultimately recommended that patients assigned to stop statins discuss restarting them with their doctors.

Noninferiority does not mean the two strategies produced identical outcomes.

Can stopping statins be appropriate? Absolutely. Frailty, life expectancy, adverse effects, and individual cardiovascular risk matter.

But stopping simply because someone turned 70? I wouldn’t recommend that.

Now I want your opinion: If a study showed more heart attacks, strokes, and deaths after stopping a medication, would you consider stopping it?

Reference: Rodondi et al. STREAM. NEJM. November 2026. DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2606634.

Stopping statins after 70 raised events from 9.2% to 12.7% in the STREAM trial

Nov 16, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 5 Statins

Shared summary of the STREAM randomized trial in adults 70+ on statins for primary prevention. Those told to stop had more heart attacks, strokes or deaths than those who continued, yet the trial formally met its noninferiority margin because that margin allowed a small loss of event-free time. Meeting noninferiority does not show that stopping carried no extra risk, and stopping brought no measurable gain in quality of life, muscle pain or falls.

STAREE: atorvastatin cut major CV events 30% in adults 70+, not disability-free survival

Aug 29, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 4 ↩ 1 Statins

A shared summary of the STAREE trial presented at ESC 2026: 9,971 adults 70+ without CVD, diabetes or dementia received atorvastatin 40 mg or placebo for a median 5.9 years. Major cardiovascular events fell 30% (6.0% vs 8.3%) but disability-free survival did not improve significantly.

STAREE: statins reduced events 30% in healthy adults 70+, NNT 37, no dementia increase

Aug 29, 2026 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 2 ↩ 1 Statins

A Vital Human summary of STAREE: 9,971 adults averaging about 75 years, atorvastatin 40 mg versus placebo for a median 5.9 years. Major CV events fell 30% (HR 0.70) with an NNT of 37, but no significant difference in overall mortality or disability-free survival and no increase in dementia.

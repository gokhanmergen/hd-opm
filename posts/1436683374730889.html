Black tea improved artery function in a Berlin trial, but adding milk erased the benefit

A European Heart Journal study (2007) from Charite Berlin had 16 healthy postmenopausal women drink black tea, tea with skimmed milk, or water on three days. Flow-mediated dilation improved with plain tea but the benefit disappeared with milk. Rat artery experiments showed tea boosts nitric oxide and milk caseins block it. Shared from BioPulse · original post

Black tea made arteries work better in a hospital trial. Add a splash of milk and the effect disappeared.

Cardiologists at the Charité hospital in Berlin ran the test and published it in the European Heart Journal in 2007. Sixteen healthy postmenopausal women came in on three separate days. On one they drank half a litre of freshly brewed black tea. On another, the same tea with skimmed milk making up a tenth of the cup. On the third, plain boiled water.

Before and two hours after each drink, the team measured flow-mediated dilation with ultrasound. It shows how well the main artery in the arm widens when blood rushes through it, and researchers use it as one of the earliest markers of blood vessel health.

Plain tea clearly improved the result compared with water. With milk, the benefit was completely blunted.

The team then went hunting for the reason using rat arteries and the cells that line blood vessels. Tea relaxed the vessels by boosting nitric oxide. Milk blocked it, and when they tested milk's proteins one at a time, the caseins turned out to be responsible. They appear to latch onto the catechins, the plant compounds credited with much of tea's protective effect.

Senior researcher Verena Stangl suggested this might help explain why tea drinking hadn't shown the expected heart benefit in Britain, where milk goes in almost every cup.

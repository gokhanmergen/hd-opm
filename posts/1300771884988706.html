Very low-carb diets rarely push A1C below 5 because of physiological insulin resistance

Louis Nervegna · May 25, 2026 · ♥ 11 reactions · 11 comments · original Facebook post
Low-carb, keto & carnivoreInsulin resistance & diabetes
AI summary & key facts

Louis argues that in people who already have low A1C, severe carb restriction makes muscles temporarily insulin resistant to spare glucose for heart and brain, so blood sugar can rise rather than fall. This 'adaptive glucose sparing' or 'pseudo-diabetes' also explains why people on long-term keto often fail glucose tolerance tests. Personal observation and physiology reasoning rather than a cited trial.

  • Rarely sees A1C below 5 on very low carb diets
  • Adaptive glucose sparing: muscles become insulin resistant to preserve glucose
  • Louis's A1C is 4.7 eating whole grains at most meals
  • Long-term ketogenic dieters often fail oral glucose tolerance tests

I have rarely seen anyone on a very low carb diet achieve an A1C lower than 5.

There is some basic physiology involved that makes this difficult.

When carbs are severely restricted in someone with low A1C to begin with, the muscles become temporally insulin resistant. This occurs in order to preserve precious glucose for the heart and brain. It is a basic survival mechanism, which is sometimes referred to as “adaptive glucose sparing” or “physiological insulin resistance”. This mechanism can cause blood sugar to go up, not down, assuming the person already started with very low blood sugar to begin with.

You could argue that the reduction in postprandial glucose spikes on a very low carb diet makes up for this increase in blood glucose due “physiological insulin resistance”. But in a person with very low A1C to begin with, these glucose spikes are already small and short in duration. Making them even smaller doesn’t affect average blood sugar very much.

On the other hand, in a person with high glucose to start, this is not true. There is already plenty of glucose available in the blood for the heart and brain. These people already have muscles that are insulin resistant. So this survival mechanism doesn’t kick in to such a large degree. This is a completely different situation.

I eat whole grains at nearly every meal, and my A1C is 4.7. If I restricted carbs, it is very possible that my blood sugar would go up, by the mechanisms just described. In that case, I would be making my blood sugar worse on a low carb diet, not better.

Addendum:

In severe cases of adaptive glucose sparing and physiological insulin resistance, the term “pseudo-diabetes” is also sometimes used.

This is why people on long-term ketogenic diets often fail oral glucose tolerance tests, even though they are not actually diabetic. They have a reversible (benevolent) pseudo-diabetes. This pseudo-diabetes makes it very hard to achieve an A1C less than 5.

The geroscientist Mikhail Blogosklonny argued that this is also what happens with use of the drug rapamycin, which mimics many of the physiological effects of calorie restriction, fasting, and carbohydrate restriction.

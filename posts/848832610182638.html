Late-life folate restriction reduced biosynthesis without harming healthspan in mice

Eating Less of Vitamin B9 Could Boost Longevity

(based on research in Yeast, Worms & Mice -- not in humans)

This study examined the effects of limiting dietary folate intake late in life on healthspan in mice. Starting at 52 weeks of age, half of the C57BL/6J mice were switched from a standard diet to one lacking folate and choline. The researchers measured various health metrics, metabolites, and gene expression over the following 68 weeks. Surprisingly, mice on the folate-restricted diet maintained or even increased their weight and did not develop anemia or other adverse health outcomes compared to control mice. Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed signatures of reduced anabolic processes in the folate-restricted animals, particularly decreased protein synthesis pathways.

The results suggest that limiting folate intake later in life may have metabolic benefits and promote healthy aging, despite the known importance of adequate folate during early development. The researchers propose this could be an example of antagonistic pleiotropy, where high folate intake is advantageous early in life but potentially detrimental later on. They note several limitations of the study, including the use of only one inbred mouse strain and a relatively small sample size. However, the findings raise intriguing questions about optimal folate intake across the lifespan and potential new avenues for promoting longevity through modulation of one-carbon metabolism pathways.

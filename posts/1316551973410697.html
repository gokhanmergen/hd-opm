Professional soccer players have average blood glucose levels of around 160 mg/dL during matches. Some have average glucose levels as high as 180-200 mg/dL.

They experience no harm from this.

To the contrary, the net benefit of the exercise is positive. Most of these professional soccer players have very low A1C, despite the big glucose spikes during matches.

The claim that healthy insulin-sensitive people experience harm from short transient increases in blood sugar should be viewed with suspicion.

There is little credible evidence to support this claim when the glucose increases remain within the normal physiological range.

What really matters is the long-term effect that exercise and foods have on insulin sensitivity and glucose control. That is what determines risk.

Professional soccer players have blood sugar levels in the diabetic range… at least temporarily.

A recent study tracked @realmadrid players using continuous glucose monitors during training and matches. Active players averaged ~159 mg/dL (8.8mmol/L) during matches, with some hitting 180-200 mg/dL. In a fasting, resting person, that’s the diabetic range.
But these are some of the fittest athletes on the planet. So what’s going on?

During intense exercise, hormones like adrenaline signal the liver to release glucose to fuel performance. Their blood sugar rose because their bodies were doing exactly what they were supposed to do; and that rise was temporary, not chronic.

This is why obsessing over every “glucose spike” after eating fruit or oatmeal misses the bigger picture. Temporary increases after eating carbs or exercising are completely normal physiology (for differing reasons). What is concerning is chronically elevated blood glucose, not brief rises in healthy people.

Both exercise and foods like fruit and oatmeal are consistently linked to a LOWER risk of type 2 diabetes. Don’t let wellness influencers selling you CGMs and supplements convince you otherwise.

Shared from Healthy Harbor · original post

A published medical case report described an 82-year-old man with Alzheimer’s disease who had experienced a steady cognitive decline for five years. At baseline, his Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 20/30, consistent with mild dementia. His symptoms included memory loss, confusion, low mood, and reduced social engagement. He was taking standard Alzheimer’s medications (donepezil and memantine), which had not stopped disease progression. He later underwent a single fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) using stool from his cognitively healthy wife — the procedure was performed to treat recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, not dementia.

Two months after the FMT, clinicians documented a marked cognitive change: his MMSE score increased to 26, placing him back in the normal range. By six months, his score further improved to 29, with sustained gains in memory, mood, and social interaction. Notably, he was again able to remember his daughter’s birthday, engage more actively in conversations, and show improved affect. No Alzheimer’s medications were changed during this period, leading the authors to suggest that altering the gut microbiome may have directly influenced brain function via the gut–brain axis, while emphasizing this was a single human case report, not a proven treatment.

Minnesota Coronary Experiment did not show that omega-6 fats cause heart disease

The Minnesota Coronary Experiment— a clinical trial conducted over 50 years ago— did not show that omega 6 fats cause heart disease.

I hear this claim repeated over and over again on social media. It is not true.

The results of the Minnesota Coronary Experiment were published in 1989. They showed that omega 6 fats did not increase the risk of cardiovascular events, cardiovascular deaths, or total mortality.

Quoted directly from the journal publication: (link below)

“For the entire study population, no differences between the treatment and control groups were observed for cardiovascular events, cardiovascular deaths, or total mortality. A favorable trend for all these end-points occurred in some younger age groups.”

Paper · Arteriosclerosis · 1989Test of effect of lipid lowering by diet on cardiovascular risk. The Minnesota Coronary Survey.The Minnesota Coronary Survey was a 4.5-year, open enrollment, single end-time double-blind, randomized clinical trial that was conducted in six Minnesota state mental hospitals and one nursing home. It involved 4393 institutionalized men and 4664 institutionalized women. There was a paper published in 2016 in the BMJ that attempted to re-analyze the data from this trial. This paper included no new data that changed the main conclusions from the 1989 paper. The paper also had major flaws in the analysis of the new data that it did include.

You can read about the problems with that 2016 paper in the Harvard article linked to below.

This Harvard article also gives some general background info describing the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, if you have never heard of it before. (It has largely been forgotten about because it is so old.)

The bottom line, as quoted from the Harvard article:

“The main results of the MCE were published by Frantz et al. (2) in 1989 with all of the aforementioned limitations, and no differences were reported between the treatment and control groups for cardiovascular events, cardiovascular deaths, or total mortality. The report of “recovered” data by Ramsden et al. (1) in BMJ [2016] adds no new data regarding these conclusions.”

To summarize, the 2016 paper adds no new data that changes the main conclusions from the original 1989 paper: Omega 6 fats did not increase cardiovascular events, cardiovascular deaths, or total mortality, when directly comparing the randomized treatment and control groups.

The controversy comes in because the 2016 paper included a post-hoc observational (non-randomized) analysis of the participants in the trial using some additional data not included in the original 1989 paper. This observational study associated lower cholesterol levels in the original study with higher risk of death. This same association has shown up in other observational studies, and the explanation has generally been reverse causality. There were major problems with this particular observational analysis, e.g. the causes of death were not recorded and are not known, so it is impossible to properly account for reverse causality. It also has to be looked at in the context of all the observational studies on omega 6 and heart disease which have been published to date. These number in the hundreds. In totality, these observational studies show a strong inverse association between omega 6 and heart disease.

Quoted from the Harvard article:

“The causes of death in the BMJ paper are not known, which makes evaluation of total mortality in non-randomized analysis relating degree of cholesterol reduction to death particularly uninformative. A small amount of new but incomplete data on autopsies were included, however the average follow up to autopsy was less than one year from baseline, making it unlikely that any benefit on gross autopsy findings for atherosclerosis would be seen.”

Hospital deaths from severe first heart attacks in adults 18-54 rose 57% from 2011 to 2022

A JAHA study reported by the Wall Street Journal found in-hospital deaths from severe first heart attacks rose in younger US adults. Louis notes overall US heart attack mortality has fallen about 5x since the 1950s, so the recent rise, likely partly obesity-driven, is a disturbing reversal.

Death rates from heart attacks in the USA have been dropping for decades.

They have dropped by 5x since the 1950’s: ↪ US heart disease death rate fell about 5-fold, from over 500 to almost 100 per 100,000.

But in the last 10-20 years, there has been a disturbing increase in deaths from heart attacks among younger people.

This appears to be part of a broader trend of declining health among younger people in the USA, with similar increases seen in deaths from other age-related diseases (most notably colon cancer).

The obesity crisis among younger people in the USA is likely playing a major role.

Research cited

  • Younger adult in-hospital deaths from severe first MI · cohort · Journal of the American Heart Association
Shared from The Wall Street Journal · original post

The proportion of adults ages 18 to 54 who died in a hospital of a severe first heart attack rose 57% between 2011 and 2022, according to a new study in the Journal of the American Heart Association.

Most of those who died were men, but women died at higher rates than men.

The study offers the latest evidence of worsening health among younger U.S. adults, including deaths from conditions traditionally tied to aging, such as heart disease and cancer.

Poorer health among younger adults is one reason that heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the U.S., despite decades of medical advances that have saved millions of lives from heart attacks and other life-threatening cardiac events.

Risk factors driving the increase included diabetes, chronic kidney disease and drug use, the study found. Low income could also play a role, if a person can’t afford to get treatment.

A higher percentage of women than men had diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease and were of low income, the study found.

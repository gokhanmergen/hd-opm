Statin absolute risk reduction is small over years but can exceed 10% over a lifetime

Louis Nervegna · Nov 10, 2025 · ♥ 20 reactions · 25 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that short statin trials show only 1-2% absolute risk reduction because baseline risk over a few years is only 2-5%, the same as diet and exercise trials. Over a lifetime, with a 20% baseline risk of heart attack, cohort and Mendelian randomization data suggest cumulative reductions of 10% or more.

  • Lifetime absolute risk of heart attack is about 20% (1 in 5 Americans)
  • Long-term statin use may cut absolute lifetime risk by 10% or more, roughly halving it
  • Short trials (about 5 years) understate cumulative benefit
  • Shared graph: a 39 mg/dL LDL reduction is highlighted

Absolute Risk & Statins

There’s a lot people in these heart disease groups online who are fond of quoting the statistic that many statin trials only show reductions in absolute risk of about 1-2%.

What they are not telling you is this:

  • These trials only run for a few years.
  • The absolute risk of having a heart attack or stroke is only around 2-5% in just a few years, even for higher risk people.
  • So reducing absolute risk by 1-2% during that short timeframe is excellent.
  • If you look at trials of diet and exercise, they also only show absolute risk reductions of around 1-2% after a few years. (Again, this is because the absolute risk of having a heart attack or stroke is only around 2-5% in just a few years, even for higher risk people.)
  • No matter what you do, you are only going to be able to reduce absolute risk by a few percent in a short number years. That’s because the absolute risk of having a heart attack or stroke in that short time is only a few percent to begin with. You can’t reduce absolute risk less than zero!
  • Even a complete CURE for heart disease will only show an absolute risk reduction of 2-5% after a few years! Again, that is because you can’t reduce absolute risk to less than zero.

The picture looks very different in the long term…

Over an entire lifetime, the absolute risk of having a heart attack is around 20%.

(One in five Americans will have a heart attack in their lifetime.)

Someone who takes a statin for a large part of their adult life can reduce this absolute risk by as much as 10% or more— just from taking the statin. We know this from both long term prospective cohort studies and Mendelian randomization studies:

↪ LDL down 39 mg/dL: 20%+ fewer heart attacks in RCTs, 30%+ in cohorts, 50%+ genetically

The benefits of statins on absolute risk reduction are CUMULATIVE. During the first few years, the absolute risk reduction is only a few percent. But over the course of a lifetime, it can exceed 10%.

That 10% number is a huge deal when you consider that the lifetime absolute risk of a heart attack itself is only around 20%. Cutting your lifetime risk from 20% to 10% means cutting your chances of a heart attack in HALF!

—————-

Any bias towards quoting relative risk to the general public is probably explained by fear that the general public will misunderstand absolute risk. For example, there is probably a fear that the general public will misunderstand that a 1% absolute risk reduction is just as impressive as a 33% relative risk reduction in a trial lasting only a few years where the absolute risk of having a heart attack is only 3% to begin with. They say the exact same thing. Both are very good results.

I think this fear is very justified given what I see online. Math illiteracy is very real. People do not understand what absolute risk truly means, and are being scammed by quacks and charlatans to think a 1% reduction is bad in my example, when in fact it is very good for a trial lasting only a few years.

Shared from Louis Nervegna · original post

Randomized controlled trials with statins only run for about 5 years. To understand the risk reduction of statins over longer periods of time, we must look at prospective cohort studies and Mendelian (genetic) randomization studies.

This graph summarizes all of this data in one place.

The yellow lines (vertical/horizontal) on the graph show that reducing your LDL by 39mg/dL— achievable by a very low dose statin— potentially reduces your risk of heart attack (compared to placebo or matched controls) by:

  • over 20% after a 5 year period (the red line, from randomized controlled trials).
  • over 30% after a 12 year period (the green line, from prospective cohort studies).
  • over 50% after a 52 year period (the blue line, from Mendelian randomization studies).

Note that the x-axis represents a change in LDL, not an absolute value of LDL. So the difference between the value of 78mg/dL and 0 mg/dL on the x-axis might correspond to a difference between 178mg/dL and 100mg/dL, for example, or any other combination that represents this difference. The y-axis also represents a percentage change in risk.

—-

How does this translate into absolute risk reduction?

1 in 5 people in the USA will have a heart attack in their lifetimes. So the average American has an absolute risk of 20% of having a heart attack during their entire lives. This is why heart disease is the #1 killer in the USA.

Dropping your LDL by 39mg/L potentially cuts this risk by half as explained above (blue line), if you do it for a long time.

So your absolute risk of a heart attack potentially drops from 20% to 10% over your entire lifetime.

All by just taking a very low dose statin and starting fairly young.

In my case, 10mg of atorvastatin is enough to drop my LDL by around 39 mg/dL. If I stick with this for the rest of my life, I will be potentially dropping my absolute risk of a heart attack from 20% to 10%. Not bad for something that takes me 30 seconds to take each day and causes me zero side effects.

Combine this low dose statin with all the other health interventions that I do, and I am fairly confident that I can get my lifetime absolute risk of a heart attack to near zero.

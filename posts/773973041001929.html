Why insulin resistance lowers HDL, and why improving insulin sensitivity beats chasing HDL

Louis Nervegna · Mar 21, 2024 · ♥ 5 reactions · 0 comments · original Facebook post
LDL, ApoB & lipid panelInsulin resistance & diabetes
AI summary & key facts

Louis lists six mechanisms by which insulin resistance lowers HDL: impaired HDL maturation (lower LCAT), reduced apoA-I production, faster HDL catabolism (hepatic lipase, CETP), altered HDL composition and function, higher triglycerides making small dense HDL, and inflammation and oxidative stress. He concludes the best way to raise HDL is improving insulin sensitivity (lose visceral fat, exercise, cut refined starch and sugar), since insulin resistance is causal in heart disease while large RCTs suggest HDL itself is probably not.

  • Mechanisms: lower LCAT, lower apoA-I, higher hepatic lipase and CETP activity, altered HDL function, high triglycerides, inflammation
  • Best ways to raise HDL: lose visceral fat, exercise, cut refined starch/sugar
  • Large RCTs suggest HDL (ApoA-I) is probably not directly causal

Why does insulin resistance lower HDL?

Below is a summary of some of the mechanisms involved.

These mechanisms collectively result in decreased HDL production, increased HDL clearance, and impaired HDL function, leading to lower circulating levels of HDL cholesterol in individuals with insulin resistance.

The best way to increase HDL is probably interventions that improve insulin sensitivity like losing visceral fat, exercise, and cutting out refined starch/sugar. The reason this is probably the best approach is because we know that insulin resistance is causal in heart disease, whereas large RCT’s seem to show that HDL (ApoA-1) per se is probably not directly causal.

Mechanisms involved:

  1. Impaired HDL maturation and metabolism:

Insulin resistance disrupts the normal maturation process of HDL particles.

It reduces the activity of the enzyme lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), which is responsible for converting free cholesterol into cholesteryl esters, a crucial step in HDL maturation.

  1. Decreased apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I) production:

ApoA-I is the primary protein component of HDL particles.

Insulin resistance can reduce the production of apoA-I in the liver, leading to fewer HDL particles being formed.

  1. Increased HDL catabolism:

Insulin resistance promotes the activity of hepatic lipase, an enzyme that breaks down HDL particles, leading to their faster clearance from the bloodstream.

It also increases the activity of cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP), which facilitates the transfer of cholesteryl esters from HDL to other lipoproteins, effectively depleting HDL particles.

  1. Altered HDL composition and function:

Insulin resistance can modify the composition and structure of HDL particles, making them less efficient in their primary function of reverse cholesterol transport (removing cholesterol from tissues and transporting it to the liver for excretion).

The HDL particles may become smaller, and denser, and more prone to clearance from the bloodstream.

  1. Increased triglyceride levels:

Insulin resistance often leads to elevated triglyceride levels, which can contribute to the formation of smaller, denser HDL particles that are more susceptible to clearance and have reduced cholesterol efflux capacity.

  1. Inflammation and oxidative stress:

Insulin resistance is associated with increased inflammation and oxidative stress, which can directly impair HDL function and metabolism, further contributing to lower HDL levels.

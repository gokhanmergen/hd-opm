U-shaped mortality curves for cholesterol, BMI, A1C and BP reflect reverse causality

Louis Nervegna · May 11, 2025 · ♥ 16 reactions · 12 comments · original Facebook post
How to read researchLDL, ApoB & lipid panelMyths, misinformation & controversies
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains why observational studies show higher mortality at both low and high LDL or total cholesterol: the same U-curves appear for BMI, A1C and blood pressure, and illnesses like cancer lower these values. RCTs and Mendelian randomization show lower LDL causes lower mortality, so high LDL does not protect.

  • Observational U-curves occur for cholesterol, BMI, A1C and blood pressure
  • Reverse causality: cancer lowers LDL and BMI; liver/kidney disease lowers A1C; uncontrolled diabetes lowers BP
  • Dozens of RCTs and MR studies show lower LDL causes lower mortality

Not a day goes by that some low carb health influencer doesn’t try to claim that high LDL magically protects you from dying.

What they don’t tell you is that these same U-shaped curves show up with lots of health parameters, not just LDL.

It’s funny how they never claim that high A1C, high blood pressure, or high BMI also protect you from dying. Because there are well established U-curves for these as well.

All of these U-curves are due to reverse causality and similar issues with observational studies.

There are now dozens of randomized controlled trials and mendellian randomization stidies that show lower LDL causes lower risk of dying.

U-shaped curves happen with lots of health-related parameters, not just cholesterol.

This is a very common phenomenon in epidemiology.

You see it with cholesterol, BMI, A1C, blood pressure, just to name a few.

Don’t be fooled by this. The consensus explanation is reverse causality.

Do you really think the optimal BMI is 28, per observational studies? That the optimal A1C is 6 to 8.5, per observational studies? That the optimal systolic blood pressure is 140 to 150, per observational studies?

Of course not.

These look optimal in observational studies because of reverse causality.

For the same reason, the optimal total cholesterol is not 220 to 240.

Note: Reverse causality means that increased risk of dying (e.g. illness) causes the parameter in question to go down. As opposed to the other way around. For example, cancer is well known to cause lower LDL and BMI. Liver or kidney diseases cause lower A1C. Uncontrolled diabetes causes lower blood pressure.

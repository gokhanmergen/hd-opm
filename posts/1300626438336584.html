Mendelian randomization shows genetically lower LDL does not raise cancer risk

Louis Nervegna · May 25, 2026 · ♥ 3 reactions · 0 comments · original Facebook post

AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that old observational links between low LDL and cancer are largely reverse causality. MR studies find genetically lowered LDL does not increase overall cancer risk, and genetically higher LDL is linked to more breast and prostate cancer; statin proxies lower colorectal cancer risk.

  • No causal association of genetically predicted LDL with overall cancer risk
  • Genetically raised LDL-C associated with increased breast and prostate cancer
  • HMGCR (statin) proxies associated with lower colorectal cancer risk
  • Genetically higher LDL linked to lower endometrial cancer

Low LDL and Cancer
Observational studies historically linked low LDL cholesterol to an increased cancer risk. However, modern Mendelian Randomization (MR) studies—which use genetic variants as lifelong proxies—have largely debunked this. They confirm that genetically lowered LDL does not increase cancer risk, and in some contexts, it can even be protective.

The relationship between LDL-C and cancer, as evaluated by MR methodologies, reveals several specific nuances:

  • Overall Cancer Risk: When looking at total cancer incidence across large cohorts, genetically-predicted LDL cholesterol levels (and gene variants that mimic lipid-lowering drugs like statins) show no significant causal association with overall cancer risk.

  • Breast and Prostate Cancers: Large-scale MR studies (utilizing targets like HMGCR and PCSK9) have actually found that genetically raised LDL-C is causally associated with an increased risk of breast and prostate cancers.
  • Colorectal Cancer: Genetic proxies for statins (HMGCR) have been found to significantly decrease the risk of colorectal cancer. However, the data can be gene-specific; some variants mimicking other drug targets (like PCSK9) have shown distinct, often site-specific variations.

Why the Disconnect?

The reason genetically decreased LDL does not cause cancer is that the low LDL levels seen in observational studies are often the consequence of undiagnosed, preclinical cancer, rather than the cause of it (reverse causality). When MR studies remove this reverse causality, researchers find that lifelong low LDL-C is perfectly safe regarding cancer.

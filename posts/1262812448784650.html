ACC/ESC consensus statement on abnormal cardiovascular findings in masters athletes

Louis Nervegna · Apr 06, 2026 · ♥ 10 reactions · 14 comments · original Facebook post
Exercise & fitnessHeart rhythm & heart failure
AI summary & key facts

Louis highlights a new joint EAPC/ESC and ACC clinical consensus statement offering guidance for long-term high-volume exercisers over 35-40, covering atrial fibrillation, arrhythmias, coronary atherosclerosis, aortic dilatation, myocardial fibrosis and exercise-induced arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. Notes that very high exercise volumes over years may have cardiovascular downsides.

  • Covers AF, bradyarrhythmias, ventricular arrhythmias, coronary atherosclerosis, aortic dilatation, myocardial fibrosis, exercise-induced arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy

Major cardiology associations around the world are increasingly recognizing the emerging evidence suggesting that intense physical exercise performed in high volume over many years, may have downsides when it comes to cardiovascular health.

They are now providing official clinical guidelines on how to handle this unique set of patients (hyper-exercisers over the age of 35-40yo), which they refer to as “masters athletes”.

This consensus clinical statement from the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) was just published.

Quoted from the consensus statement:

“Exercise training improves cardiovascular health and reduces the risk for future cardiovascular events and mortality.

However, emerging evidence suggests that Masters athletes may have a higher prevalence of cardiovascular abnormalities, such as atrial arrhythmias, CORONARY ATHEROSCLEROSIS, aortic dilatation, and myocardial fibrosis, compared to their less active peers.

The clinical management of Masters athletes may be challenging as available guidelines for such conditions are generally based on data derived from symptomatic sedentary patients, limiting their applicability to highly trained individuals. Other unique challenges in the clinical assessment of Masters athletes include differences in symptomatic presentations compared to sedentary individuals, potential resistance to the initiation of pharmacologic treatment, and the increasing availability of consumer wearable health data that may provide relevant information on their cardiovascular health status.”

My note:

This is an inconvenient truth that has been ignored by most of the influencers and authors of pop health books who claim to be experts on fitness and “longevity”. However, to anyone who has followed the cardiology literature over the last ten years, it is not a surprise. The evidence has been slowly building for some time. The case is now strong enough that the major cardiology organizations have no choice but to officially acknowledge it.

I want to be clear about this: The preponderance of evidence still suggests a net benefit to people who fall into this “master athlete” class, especially with regards to total mortality. But there are also unmistakable downsides that might cause serious harm to certain subsets of this group. Understanding exactly who these subsets might be is a major goal for future research.

Scientists found bacteria in artery plaques that can trigger heart attacks — and vaccines could prevent it.

A surprising new study suggests that heart attacks may, in part, be infectious events triggered by hidden bacteria in the arteries. Researchers at Tampere University discovered that cholesterol-rich plaques in coronary arteries can harbor bacterial biofilms—dense, jelly-like structures that shield bacteria from both the immune system and antibiotics. These biofilms can lie dormant for decades, undetected and symptom-free, until a viral illness or other trigger activates them. Once awakened, the bacteria multiply, fueling inflammation that weakens plaque walls. This process can rupture the plaque, block blood flow, and ultimately cause a heart attack.

The findings provide direct evidence that bacterial DNA from oral microbes exists within atherosclerotic plaques, reinforcing long-standing suspicions about infection’s role in heart disease. Scientists even developed antibodies that revealed biofilm structures in arterial tissue and detected bacteria released during heart attacks. These results suggest that preventing myocardial infarction could one day involve vaccines or therapies designed to target biofilm-associated bacteria. If validated in further research, this approach could transform how doctors diagnose, treat, and even prevent the world’s leading cause of death.

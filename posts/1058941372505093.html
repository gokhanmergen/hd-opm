No RCT shows any food causes cancer; red meat evidence comes from prospective cohorts

Louis Nervegna · Jul 01, 2025 · ♥ 4 reactions · 4 comments · original Facebook post
How to read researchMeat, eggs & dairy
AI summary & key facts

Louis argues that cancer and trans fat causation evidence in humans comes mostly from prospective cohort studies since RCTs cannot run for decades. He applies this reasoning to the red meat and cancer debate.

  • Zero human RCTs show smoking causes lung cancer or trans fats cause heart disease
  • Prospective cohorts are the main high-quality design for long-latency disease

Red meat and cancer

There are zero randomized controlled trials in humans that show any food causes cancer.

There are zero randomized controlled trials in humans that show smoking causes lung cancer.

There are zero randomized controlled trials in humans that show trans fats cause heart disease.

The evidence for these links in humans comes primarily from prospective cohort studies. Prospective cohort studies are the only high quality study form that can provide evidence for potential causes of diseases that take decades to develop. It is impossible to study this in randomized controlled trials:

Reliable medical knowledge does not require RCTs: smoking and trans fats as examples

Jan 08, 2024 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 17 · 💬 9 ↩ 1 How to read research

Louis argues that long RCTs are often unethical or impractical for diseases that take decades to develop, and that no human RCT showed smoking causes lung cancer or trans fats cause heart disease. Instead, causality is established by combining high-quality prospective cohorts, short-term biomarker RCTs and animal mechanistic studies, applying Bradford-Hill criteria, as with the Framingham Heart Study. He says much nutrition knowledge was built this way, so dismissing it for lacking long RCTs is mistaken.

Harvard review: fat not clearly linked to cancer; red and processed meat raise colon risk

Dec 20, 2024 · Louis Nervegna ♥ 4 · 💬 2 ↩ 1 Fats & oils (saturated fat, seed oils, omega-6, olive oil)

Louis quotes the Harvard Nutrition Source summary on dietary fat and cancer. Total fat percentage and fat types show no clear association with cancer, and the Women's Health Initiative low-fat trial did not reduce breast or colon cancer. Red and processed meat do increase colon cancer risk, and animal fat in young women was linked to higher breast cancer risk in Nurses' Health Study II. He notes a later Harvard study updates this.

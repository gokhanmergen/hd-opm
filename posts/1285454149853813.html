Lewy body dementia is the second most common dementia, on a spectrum with Parkinson's

Louis Nervegna · May 07, 2026 · ♥ 9 reactions · 6 comments · original Facebook post
Brain health & dementia
AI summary & key facts

Louis explains that Lewy body dementia is often misdiagnosed as Alzheimer's, is closely related to Parkinson's, and is marked by alpha-synuclein clumping in the brain. The post was prompted by Ted Turner's death and has little cardiovascular relevance.

  • Second most common dementia after Alzheimer's
  • Hallmark is abnormal alpha-synuclein clumping
  • More than 1 million Americans affected

Lewy body dementia is the second most common form of dementia after Alzheimer’s, yet most people have never heard of it.

It is commonly misdiagnosed as Alzheimer’s by physicians who do not properly understand it.

Ted Turner’s recent death is raising awareness.

Lewy body dementia can be roughly thought of as a form of Parkinson’s disease, with symptoms being primary cognitive (and involving the cerebral cortex) instead of physical.

Parkinson’s and Lewy body dementia are on the same spectrum. They are different manifestations of the same underlying disease processes.

The classic hallmark of both diseases is abnormal clumping of the protein alpha-synuclein in the brain, which ultimately leads to destruction of neurons. In Parkinson’s, it is primarily the dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra that are affected. But in Lewy body dementia, this destruction extends more broadly to other regions of the brain, especially the cerebral cortex.

Unlike Alzheimer’s, the first symptoms of Lewy body dementia are often related to executive function as opposed to short-term memory loss, i.e. problems handling complex tasks. Symptoms like hallucinations, delusions, and REM sleep disorder (talking and moving in your sleep) occur early in the disease, unlike Alzheimer’s where they typically occur much later. There are often accompanying physical symptoms that occur early-on like slowness of movement, hunching, and shuffled gate, similar to Parkinson’s but milder. Most people with Lewy body dementia never develop tremors, like you typically see in Parkinson’s, but this can also occur in some.

There is now an accurate skin biopsy test that can be used to properly diagnose Lewy body dementia. This test is still very new, and is offered primarily by large research hospitals. It tests for the presence of abnormal (phosphorylated) alpha-synuclein in nerve cells of the skin.
